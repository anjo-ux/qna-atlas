1. Pediatric Ganglion
A 5-year-old girl is brought to the office by her father, who noticed a lump on the back of her right wrist approximately 3 months ago. She has had no pain and uses the hand normally for writing and for gymnastics. Physical examination shows a soft, mobile, 1.2-cm mass over the dorsal wrist that transilluminates. Wrist range of motion is full and symmetric, and there is no tenderness at the scapholunate interval. Plain radiographs show no abnormalities. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A) Reassurance and serial observation B) Aspiration with corticosteroid injection C) Open excision including resection of the capsular stalk D) Arthroscopic resection E) Closed rupture by manual compression
The correct response is Option A.
Pediatric ganglion cysts behave very differently from those in adults, and the natural history strongly favors nonoperative management. Longitudinal series report that the large majority of wrist ganglia in children resolve spontaneously — frequently within 1 to 2 years — without any intervention. In a child with a classic presentation (soft, mobile, transilluminating mass over the dorsal wrist, full motion, normal radiographs), the diagnosis is clinical, and advanced imaging is not required.
Open excision (Option C) commits an otherwise well child to general anesthesia, a scar, and a recurrence rate that in pediatric series has been reported as high as 30 to 40% — no better than simply waiting. Arthroscopic resection (Option D) is a technique for symptomatic or recurrent adult ganglia and is not a first-line pediatric option. Aspiration with steroid injection (Option B) is poorly tolerated by young children, does not alter the natural history, and carries high recurrence. Closed rupture (Option E) — historically "Bible therapy" — is an abandoned, traumatic maneuver with high recurrence and no role in modern practice.
Surgery should be reserved for lesions that are painful, functionally limiting, persistent into adolescence with symptoms, or clinically atypical (solid, nontransilluminating, rapidly enlarging, or associated with carpal instability or neurologic findings), where an alternative diagnosis such as giant cell tumor of tendon sheath, anomalous extensor digitorum brevis manus, or a neural tumor must be excluded.

2. Blood Supply Relevant to Labiaplasty
A 32-year-old woman comes to the office because of chronic irritation and discomfort with exercise caused by protuberant labia minora. Reduction labiaplasty using a central wedge resection with a superiorly based flap is planned. Which of the following vessels provides the dominant arterial supply to the labia minora?
A) Deep external pudendal artery B) Inferior epigastric artery C) Internal pudendal artery D) Obturator artery E) Uterine artery
The correct response is Option C.
The labia minora derive their dominant arterial supply from the internal pudendal artery, a branch of the anterior division of the internal iliac artery. The internal pudendal gives off the posterior labial arteries, which enter the labium from its posterior and lateral aspect, along with anterior contributions from the dorsal artery of the clitoris. Cadaveric injection studies have shown two to five dominant vessels entering posteriorly, feeding a longitudinally oriented, richly anastomotic subdermal plexus that runs the length of the labium.
This anatomy is the reason multiple labiaplasty techniques are all reliably perfused. Both the trim (edge) technique and wedge techniques — central, inferior, or superior wedge, with either a superior or inferior pedicle — leave enough of the longitudinal plexus intact to perfuse the residual labium. Flap loss is uncommon and is more often attributable to closure under tension, aggressive de-fatting or over-thinning of the pedicle, or hematoma than to true arterial insufficiency.
The superficial and deep external pudendal arteries (Option A) arise from the femoral artery and supply the mons pubis and anterior labia majora, contributing comparatively little to the labia minora. The obturator artery (Option D) supplies the medial thigh and hip joint. The uterine artery (Option E) supplies the uterus and upper vagina. The inferior epigastric artery (Option B) supplies the anterior abdominal wall.

3. Cephalometric Landmarks (Porion and Gonion)
A 19-year-old man with a long lower face, lip incompetence, and an anterior open bite is undergoing orthognathic evaluation. As part of the lateral cephalometric analysis, the surgeon calculates the Frankfort–mandibular plane angle. Which of the following pairs of landmark sets is used to construct the two planes required for this measurement?
A) Nasion to sella, and gonion to menton B) Porion to orbitale, and gonion to menton C) Porion to nasion, and condylion to gonion D) Orbitale to anterior nasal spine, and pogonion to menton E) Sella to nasion, and point A to point B
The correct response is Option B.
The Frankfort horizontal is drawn from porion — the most superior point of the bony external auditory canal — to orbitale, the most inferior point of the infraorbital rim. The mandibular plane is drawn from gonion to menton. Gonion is the most posteroinferior point at the mandibular angle, constructed on a cephalogram as the point where the bisector of two lines (one tangent to the posterior border of the ramus, one tangent to the inferior border of the body) intersects the angle. Menton is the most inferior point of the mandibular symphysis.
The Frankfort–mandibular plane angle averages roughly 25 degrees. A high (hyperdivergent) angle accompanies a long lower face, vertical maxillary excess, steep mandibular plane, and a tendency toward anterior open bite — the pattern described here. A low (hypodivergent) angle accompanies a short lower face and deep bite.
The other landmarks in the distractors are worth distinguishing. Gnathion is the midpoint between pogonion and menton on the chin contour and substitutes for menton in some analyses (condylion–gnathion is used to express mandibular length). Pogonion is the most anterior projecting point of the chin and is used for the facial plane and SNPg. Condylionis the most superoposterior point of the condylar head. Sella–nasion is the cranial base reference line used for SNA and SNB, not for the Frankfort–mandibular plane angle.

4. Mondor Syndrome
A 41-year-old woman comes to the office 17 days after undergoing bilateral vertical mastopexy. She reports that she can feel a "tight string" beneath the skin of the right breast that pulls when she raises her arm. She is afebrile. Physical examination shows a firm, linear, mildly tender subcutaneous cord extending from the inferior pole of the right breast toward the epigastrium, with visible skin dimpling along its course when the arm is elevated. There is no surrounding erythema, fluctuance, or drainage, and the incisions are intact. Leukocyte count is within normal limits. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A) Warm compresses, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and reassurance B) A 10-day course of oral cephalexin C) Therapeutic-dose low-molecular-weight heparin for 3 months D) Duplex ultrasonography with hypercoagulable laboratory workup E) Surgical excision of the cord
The correct response is Option A.
The findings are classic for Mondor disease, a benign superficial thrombophlebitis of the thoracoepigastric, lateral thoracic, or superior epigastric venous system. It characteristically appears 2 to 4 weeks after a breast operation — most often reported after augmentation through an inframammary incision, but also after mastopexy, reduction, core needle biopsy, radiation, or trauma — and can occur spontaneously. The hallmark is a palpable, tender, linear cord with skin dimpling or retraction that becomes more apparent with arm elevation.
The condition is self-limited and typically resolves over 4 to 8 weeks. Symptomatic management with warm compresses, gentle massage, analgesics or anti-inflammatory agents, and reassurance is all that is required. Antibiotics (Option B) are not indicated, as this is not an infectious process and there are no signs of cellulitis. Systemic anticoagulation (Option C) is not warranted for superficial thrombophlebitis in this distribution and exposes a recently operated patient to unnecessary bleeding risk. Duplex ultrasonography and a hypercoagulable workup (Option D) are unnecessary for a classic postoperative presentation. Excision (Option E) is never the initial treatment and would create a new scar for a process that resolves on its own.
Two related points are worth knowing. Mondor disease arising spontaneously, without a preceding operation, biopsy, or trauma, warrants breast imaging, since a small proportion of cases are associated with an underlying carcinoma. Separately, cording in the axilla and medial arm after axillary lymph node dissection is axillary web syndrome — a related but distinct entity managed with stretching and physical therapy.

5. Interval for Locating the Superficial Branch of the Radial Nerve
A 27-year-old man has a 4-month history of burning pain over the dorsoradial forearm with paresthesias radiating into the dorsum of the thumb and first web space. Symptoms began after several months of wearing a tight metal wristband and are worsened by forearm pronation. Physical examination shows a Tinel sign approximately 8 cm proximal to the radial styloid; the Finkelstein test is negative, and there is no tenderness over the first dorsal compartment. Symptoms have not improved after 7 months of splinting, activity modification, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. Surgical decompression is planned. Release of the antebrachial fascia at which of the following intervals is most appropriate?
A) Between the abductor pollicis longus and the extensor pollicis brevis B) Between the brachioradialis and the extensor carpi radialis longus C) Between the extensor carpi radialis longus and the extensor carpi radialis brevis D) Between the extensor digitorum communis and the extensor carpi radialis brevis E) Between the flexor carpi radialis and the brachioradialis
The correct response is Option B.
This is Wartenberg syndrome, compression of the superficial branch of the radial nerve in the distal forearm. Through the proximal and mid forearm the nerve travels deep to the brachioradialis. Approximately 8 to 9 cm proximal to the radial styloid it emerges between the tendons of the brachioradialis and the extensor carpi radialis longus, piercing the antebrachial fascia to become subcutaneous. With forearm pronation the brachioradialis tendon rotates over the extensor carpi radialis longus and scissors the nerve at this transition point, which explains both the symptom pattern and the site of the Tinel sign. Decompression consists of releasing the fascia at this brachioradialis–extensor carpi radialis longus interval, extended proximally and distally past the transition zone.
The same anatomy defines where to place a subfascial radial nerve block: just proximal to where the nerve becomes subcutaneous. At the wrist, the nerve has already fanned into multiple branches and is blocked instead by subcutaneous field infiltration over the radial styloid.
The other intervals are anatomically real but wrong for this nerve. The abductor pollicis longus and extensor pollicis brevis (Option A) constitute the first dorsal compartment — the site of de Quervain tenosynovitis, which coexists with Wartenberg syndrome in a substantial minority of patients and is excluded here by the negative Finkelstein test. The extensor carpi radialis longus and brevis (Option C) form the second dorsal compartment; intersection syndrome occurs where the first compartment tendons cross over them 4 to 6 cm proximal to the Lister tubercle. The interval between the extensor digitorum communis and the extensor carpi radialis brevis (Option D) is the approach to the radial nerve and posterior interosseous nerve in the proximal forearm. The interval between the flexor carpi radialis and the brachioradialis (Option E) contains the radial artery.

6. Plane of Upper Eyelid Weight Placement
A 58-year-old woman has complete flaccid facial paralysis 14 months after resection of a vestibular schwannoma with sacrifice of the facial nerve. She has 6 mm of lagophthalmos, a poor Bell phenomenon, and punctate keratopathy despite maximal lubrication. Placement of an upper eyelid platinum weight is planned. In which of the following planes should the weight be positioned?
A) Between the tarsal plate and the palpebral conjunctiva B) Between the orbicularis oculi and the anterior surface of the tarsal plate and levator aponeurosis C) In the subcutaneous plane superficial to the orbicularis oculi D) Deep to the levator aponeurosis, superior to the upper border of the tarsus E) Within the preaponeurotic fat pad
The correct response is Option B.
The weight is placed in the pretarsal plane: deep to the orbicularis oculi and superficial to the levator aponeurosis and tarsal plate. Through a lid-crease incision the orbicularis is split, the anterior tarsal surface is exposed, and the implant is seated with its inferior edge 2 to 3 mm above the lash line, centered over the medial limbus — roughly the junction of the medial and central thirds of the lid. It is secured to tarsus with nonabsorbable suture through the implant fixation holes, and orbicularis and skin are closed over it to reduce visibility and extrusion.
Placement posterior to the tarsus (Option A) exposes the implant to the conjunctiva and cornea, risking abrasion, erosion, and extrusion. Subcutaneous placement (Option C) produces a visible, palpable bulge and has an unacceptably high extrusion rate. Positions above the upper tarsal border (Options D and E) sacrifice mechanical advantage over the lid margin, blunt the effect on closure, and produce ptosis and contour irregularity; a described modification places thin platinum chain between the septum and levator aponeurosis in very thin-skinned patients, but the pretarsal plane remains the workhorse.
Two related decisions are commonly tested. Weight is selected preoperatively by taping trial weights to the lid and choosing the lightest weight that brings the upper lid to within 2 to 4 mm of the lower lid with full corneal coverage on relaxed closure; the common error is over-weighting in pursuit of complete closure, which causes ptosis, visual field obstruction, and reoperation. Platinum is increasingly favored over gold because its higher density allows a thinner, less visible profile with lower rates of allergic reaction and capsule formation.

7. Saddle Nose Deformity
A 29-year-old woman is referred for evaluation 2 years after undergoing septoplasty elsewhere. She reports worsening nasal obstruction and progressive flattening of her nasal profile. Physical examination shows loss of dorsal height through the middle third with supratip depression, cephalic rotation of the tip, loss of tip projection, columellar retraction, and widening of the tip. Intranasal examination shows a nearly absent cartilaginous septum with a deficient caudal strut; the mucosa is intact and there is no crusting or perforation. Serologic studies are negative and she does not use intranasal drugs. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A) Injection of hyaluronic acid filler along the dorsum B) Dorsal onlay grafting with temporoparietal fascia alone C) Placement of a silicone dorsal implant D) Autologous costal cartilage reconstruction with caudal septal replacement and dorsal onlay grafting E) Bilateral conchal cartilage onlay grafting to the dorsum
The correct response is Option D.
This is a major saddle nose deformity, and the defining problem is loss of the structural L-strut, not simply a contour depression. The tell-tale findings are the associated derangements of tip support — cephalic rotation, columellar retraction, loss of projection, tip widening — which indicate that the caudal septum is no longer supporting the nose. Restoring dorsal height without re-establishing that support fails predictably: the tip abnormalities persist and the dorsum recollapses.
Autologous costal cartilage supplies both the volume and the rigidity required. Reconstruction typically combines a caudal septal replacement graft or extended spreader grafts fixed to the nasal spine and remaining septal remnant to rebuild the L-strut, with a dorsal onlay — solid carved cartilage or diced cartilage in fascia — to restore height and smooth the contour. Warping is mitigated with balanced carving, allowing carved grafts to sit before use, and K-wire stabilization when indicated.
Septal cartilage is unavailable in this patient. Conchal cartilage (Option E) is thin, curved, and insufficient in volume and strength for a structural strut; it is reasonable only for minor saddling or camouflage. Filler (Option A) and fascia-only onlay (Option B) camouflage a mild contour depression but restore no support and do not address obstruction. Alloplastic dorsal implants (Option C) in a scarred, previously operated nose with compromised soft-tissue cover carry high rates of infection, extrusion, and displacement, and provide no caudal support.
Etiologies of saddle nose worth recalling: overresection at septoplasty with an inadequate L-strut (at least 10 mm of dorsal and caudal strut should be preserved), unrecognized L-strut fracture, untreated septal hematoma or abscess with avascular necrosis of cartilage (a classic pediatric cause), trauma, intranasal cocaine use, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, relapsing polychondritis, and leprosy or congenital syphilis. Active vasculitis or ongoing cocaine use must be excluded and the disease quiescent before reconstruction is attempted.
Vascular Anomalies
Question 1
A 17-year-old boy comes to the office because of multiple firm, painless enlargements of the fingers of both hands and several soft, bluish, compressible subcutaneous masses of the forearms that swell when the arm is held dependent. He has a history of two pathologic fractures of the phalanges. Radiographs show multiple expansile lucent lesions of the phalanges and metacarpals with cortical thinning, and several phleboliths within the soft tissues. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?
A)      Klippel-Trénaunay syndrome
B)      Maffucci syndrome
C)       Ollier disease
D)      Parkes-Weber syndrome
E)      Sturge-Weber syndrome
The correct response is Option B.
The combination of multiple enchondromas and venous malformations defines Maffucci syndrome. The venous component is the discriminating finding: soft, compressible, nonpulsatile lesions that enlarge with dependency and contain phleboliths, growing proportionately with the child rather than involuting. Maffucci syndrome is nonhereditary and typically becomes clinically apparent in adolescence. The critical counseling point is malignant potential — the risk of transformation of enchondromas to chondrosarcoma approaches 30%, and there is an additional risk of intracranial and other visceral malignancies. Any enchondroma that becomes painful, enlarges rapidly, or recurs after curettage should be regarded as a possible low-grade chondrosarcoma.
Ollier disease (Option C) is multiple enchondromatosis without the vascular lesions. Klippel-Trénaunay syndrome (Option A) is the triad of capillary malformation, venous varicosities, and limb overgrowth without arteriovenous fistulae, and its principal morbidity is limb-length discrepancy. Parkes-Weber syndrome (Option D) resembles Klippel-Trénaunay but includes arteriovenous fistulae and carries a risk of high-output cardiac failure. Sturge-Weber syndrome (Option E) pairs a trigeminal-distribution capillary malformation with leptomeningeal vascular anomalies and seizures.
Question 2
A 44-year-old woman with limited cutaneous systemic sclerosis has a 3-year history of episodic pallor, cyanosis, and reperfusion hyperemia of all digits of both hands, now accompanied by constant rest pain and a nonhealing ulcer of the index fingertip. She does not smoke. Calcium channel blockade, phosphodiesterase inhibition, and topical nitrates have failed to control her symptoms. Arteriography shows patent radial and ulnar arteries with diffuse distal spasm and no discrete occlusion. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?
A)      Catheter-directed thrombolysis
B)      Injection of botulinum toxin type A perivascularly around the digital neurovascular bundles in the palm
C)       Intradermal injection of botulinum toxin type A at the wrist
D)      Stellate ganglion injection of botulinum toxin type A
E)      Ulnar artery resection with interposition vein grafting
The correct response is Option B.
Perivascular injection of botulinum toxin around the digital neurovascular bundles at the level of the distal palm is an effective, low-morbidity treatment for refractory secondary Raynaud phenomenon. Roughly 10 units are bathed around each bundle. Reported outcomes include substantial reduction in rest pain in the majority of patients and healing of chronic digital ulcers in up to half. The mechanism is incompletely understood but is thought to involve inhibition of sympathetic vasoconstrictor input, blockade of C-fiber sensory afferents, and effects on substance P signaling. The main adverse effect is transient intrinsic muscle weakness.
Injection intradermally or too proximally at the wrist (Option C) has not reproduced these results. Stellate ganglion blockade (Option D) is performed with local anesthetic, not botulinum toxin. Thrombolysis (Option A) requires a thrombotic occlusion, which arteriography excluded — the pathology here is vasospasm on a patent arterial tree, which is typical of autoimmune disease. Ulnar artery resection and grafting (Option E) is the operation for hypothenar hammer syndrome, a unilateral, occupation-related thrombosis of the ulnar artery. Surgical digital periarterial sympathectomy — stripping the adventitia from the radial, ulnar, and digital vessels — is the appropriate escalation if botulinum toxin fails.
Question 3
A 4-month-old girl is brought to the office because of a flat, well-demarcated, nonblanching pink-red patch involving the right forehead, upper eyelid, and nasal sidewall that has been present since birth and has grown only in proportion to the child. She has had two episodes of focal seizure activity. Which of the following is the most appropriate combination of additional evaluations?
A)      Abdominal ultrasonography and serum alpha-fetoprotein
B)      Coagulation studies and platelet count
C)       Echocardiography and serum brain natriuretic peptide
D)      MRI of the brain and ophthalmologic examination with tonometry
E)      Skeletal survey and serum calcium
The correct response is Option D.
A capillary malformation (port-wine stain) in a V1 distribution raises concern for Sturge-Weber syndrome, particularly when it involves the forehead and upper eyelid. The two organ systems at risk are the brain and the eye. Ipsilateral leptomeningeal vascular anomalies produce seizures, developmental delay, and hemiparesis and are best evaluated with contrast-enhanced MRI. Ipsilateral glaucoma occurs in a substantial minority of affected children, may be present at birth or develop later, and is a preventable cause of blindness — hence serial ophthalmologic examination with intraocular pressure measurement. Both Sturge-Weber syndrome and isolated nonsyndromic capillary malformations are associated with somatic GNAQ mutations.
Capillary malformations do not involute; they darken, thicken, and develop nodularity over decades, and associated soft-tissue and skeletal overgrowth is common. Pulsed-dye laser (595 nm) beginning in infancy is the treatment of choice for the cutaneous lesion.
Coagulation studies (Option B) address Kasabach-Merritt phenomenon, which occurs with kaposiform hemangioendothelioma and tufted angioma, not capillary malformations. Echocardiography (Option C) is relevant to high-flow lesions with potential for cardiac overload. Options A and E do not correspond to recognized associations.
Question 4
A 14-year-old girl comes to the office because of a 6 × 8-cm bluish, soft, compressible mass of the medial thigh that has been present since birth, enlarges when she stands, and has become increasingly painful over the past year with intermittent episodes of acute swelling and tenderness. MRI shows a T2-hyperintense, lobulated lesion with phleboliths involving subcutaneous tissue and the adductor musculature, with no flow voids. Which of the following is the most appropriate first-line treatment?
A)      Arterial embolization
B)      Oral propranolol therapy
C)       Pulsed-dye laser therapy
D)      Sclerotherapy
E)      Surgical resection
The correct response is Option D.
This is a symptomatic venous malformation, and sclerotherapy is first-line management. A sclerosant such as sodium tetradecyl sulfate, ethanol, bleomycin, or doxycycline is injected directly into the malformation, producing endothelial injury, thrombosis, fibrosis, and contraction of the abnormal channels. It is more effective and far less morbid than resection. The episodic pain and swelling described reflect localized intravascular coagulopathy and phlebothrombosis within the lesion, which also explains the phleboliths; an elevated D-dimer with normal or low fibrinogen is characteristic, and compression garments and low-molecular-weight heparin can help symptomatically.
Resection (Option E) is second-line and is reserved for small, well-circumscribed lesions or persistent symptoms after sclerotherapy. These lesions are infiltrative and poorly circumscribed, so excision is rarely complete, carries risks of bleeding and nerve injury, leaves a scar, and is frequently followed by recurrence. Embolization (Option A) treats high-flow arteriovenous malformations. Propranolol (Option B) is effective for problematic infantile hemangiomas and has no role in vascular malformations. Pulsed-dye laser (Option C) penetrates only a few millimeters and treats cutaneous staining, not a deep intramuscular lesion.
Question 5
A 6-week-old girl is brought to the office because of a raised, bright red lesion of the left cheek. The parents report that the skin appeared slightly pale with fine telangiectasias at birth and that the raised red component appeared at 3 weeks of age and has grown rapidly since. Which of the following findings would most strongly support the diagnosis of infantile hemangioma over congenital hemangioma?
A)      Absence of a bruit on auscultation
B)      Growth in proportion to the child
C)       Immunoreactivity for GLUT-1 on biopsy
D)      Presence of central ulceration
E)      Rapid response to intralesional corticosteroid injection
The correct response is Option C.
Infantile hemangioma uniquely and consistently expresses the erythrocyte-type glucose transporter GLUT-1 throughout all phases of its life cycle. Congenital hemangiomas — both the rapidly involuting (RICH) and noninvoluting (NICH) variants — are GLUT-1 negative, as are vascular malformations. The clinical history here is also typical of infantile hemangioma: a subtle premonitory mark at birth (pallor, telangiectasia, or a faint stain), followed by rapid proliferation beginning in the first weeks of life. Proliferation continues through roughly 6 to 9 months, followed by involution over years. Congenital hemangiomas, by contrast, are fully formed at birth and either involute rapidly during the first year or never involute at all.
Growth in proportion to the child (Option B) describes a vascular malformation, not either neoplasm. Ulceration (Option D) is the most common complication of infantile hemangioma but occurs in other lesions as well and is not diagnostic. Absence of a bruit (Option A) argues against a high-flow malformation but does not distinguish the two hemangiomas. Oral propranolol — not intralesional steroid (Option E) — is first-line therapy for a problematic infantile hemangioma; congenital hemangiomas do not respond to propranolol.
Question 6
A 3-week-old boy is transferred for evaluation of a rapidly enlarging, firm, indurated, deep purple lesion of the left shoulder and axilla with overlying ecchymosis and taut, shiny skin. He is irritable and pale. Laboratory studies show a platelet count of 12,000/mm3, a fibrinogen concentration of 60 mg/dL, and an elevated D-dimer level. Which of the following is the most likely underlying lesion?
A)      Arteriovenous malformation
B)      Congenital hemangioma, noninvoluting type
C)       Infantile hemangioma
D)      Kaposiform hemangioendothelioma
E)      Venous malformation
The correct response is Option D.
Profound thrombocytopenia with hypofibrinogenemia and elevated D-dimer in an infant with a firm, indurated, purpuric vascular lesion is Kasabach-Merritt phenomenon, which occurs almost exclusively with kaposiform hemangioendothelioma and tufted angioma. The lesion traps and consumes platelets, and the consumptive coagulopathy — not the mass itself — is what threatens the child’s life, with historical mortality rates of 20 to 30%. First-line pharmacologic therapy is systemic corticosteroids with vincristine; sirolimus has become an important agent and is increasingly used, sometimes as primary therapy. Platelet transfusion should be avoided except for active bleeding or before a procedure, because transfused platelets are rapidly sequestered and can worsen swelling.
An important teaching point is that the common infantile hemangioma (Option C) does not cause Kasabach-Merritt phenomenon, despite older literature that used “hemangioma” loosely. Venous malformations (Option E) cause a localized intravascular coagulopathy with elevated D-dimer and low fibrinogen but only mild thrombocytopenia, and they are soft and compressible rather than firm and indurated. Arteriovenous malformations (Option A) are warm and pulsatile with a thrill or bruit. Noninvoluting congenital hemangioma (Option B) is a stable, well-circumscribed lesion without coagulopathy.
Question 7
A 24-year-old man has a warm, pulsatile mass of the left ear and preauricular region that has been present since childhood and has enlarged markedly over the past 2 years, since a course of corticosteroid therapy for an unrelated condition. Examination shows a palpable thrill, an audible bruit, dilated overlying veins, and a 1-cm area of ulceration at the helical rim with recent episodes of bleeding. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A)      Compression therapy and observation
B)      Ligation of the feeding external carotid branches
C)       Oral sirolimus therapy alone
D)      Sclerotherapy with sodium tetradecyl sulfate
E)      Superselective embolization followed within 72 hours by wide resection and reconstruction
The correct response is Option E.
This is a Schobinger stage III arteriovenous malformation — a lesion that has progressed from quiescence (stage I) through expansion with thrill and bruit (stage II) to destruction with ulceration, pain, and hemorrhage. Stage IV adds high-output cardiac failure. Intervention is indicated. The standard approach is superselective embolization of the nidus followed by complete resection within roughly 24 to 72 hours, before recruitment of collateral inflow occurs. Embolization alone is palliative and followed by recanalization and recruitment; the goal of embolization here is to reduce intraoperative hemorrhage, not to cure. Resection must include the nidus with margins into normal tissue, and the resulting defect frequently requires flap reconstruction. Even after apparently complete excision, recurrence rates are significant, and long-term surveillance is required. Hormonal surges — puberty, pregnancy — and iatrogenic events such as partial treatment or trauma classically trigger expansion.
Proximal feeding-vessel ligation (Option B) is contraindicated: it does not treat the nidus, recruits collateral supply, and forecloses future endovascular access. Sclerotherapy (Option D) is a treatment for slow-flow lesions. Sirolimus (Option C) has activity in complex vascular anomalies but is not definitive management of a destructive, bleeding arteriovenous malformation. Observation (Option A) is appropriate only for stage I lesions.
Question 8
An 8-month-old boy is evaluated because of a soft, transilluminating, 10-cm mass of the left neck and floor of the mouth that was noted on prenatal ultrasonography. He has had three episodes of rapid painful enlargement associated with upper respiratory infections. MRI shows a multiloculated lesion with fluid-fluid levels and cysts larger than 2 cm. The airway is stable. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial treatment?
A)      Interferon alfa therapy
B)      Observation with the expectation of spontaneous involution
C)       Oral propranolol therapy
D)      Sclerotherapy with doxycycline
E)      Wide surgical resection
The correct response is Option D.
This is a macrocystic lymphatic malformation, and sclerotherapy is first-line treatment. Doxycycline, OK-432, bleomycin, and ethanol are all used; each induces an inflammatory endothelial injury with subsequent fibrosis and collapse of the cysts. Macrocystic lesions — cysts larger than about 1 to 2 cm — respond substantially better than microcystic disease, and sclerotherapy is both more effective and less morbid than resection.
Episodic painful enlargement with intercurrent infection or intralesional bleeding is characteristic and is managed with antibiotics and anti-inflammatory agents; the caregiver should be counseled that these episodes recur.
Resection (Option E) is second-line, reserved for lesions that fail sclerotherapy or are focal and well-circumscribed. It risks bleeding, nerve injury, wound breakdown, and recurrence, because complete extirpation of an infiltrative lymphatic malformation is rarely achievable. Lymphatic malformations are present at birth, grow with the child, and do not involute, so observation (Option B) is not appropriate for a symptomatic lesion. Propranolol (Option C) and interferon (Option A) target infantile hemangioma and have no efficacy against malformations. Sirolimus is a useful adjunct for extensive microcystic and combined lesions.
Question 9
A 5-week-old girl is brought to the office because of a large, segmental, plaque-like red lesion covering the right side of the face in a mandibular and cervicofacial distribution, which is proliferating. In addition to a hemangioma-directed evaluation, which of the following studies is most important to obtain in this patient?
A)      Chromosomal breakage testing
B)      Lumbosacral ultrasonography
C)       Magnetic resonance angiography of the head and neck with echocardiography
D)      Renal ultrasonography
E)      Skeletal survey
The correct response is Option C.
Large segmental facial infantile hemangiomas — as opposed to small focal lesions — should prompt evaluation for PHACE association: posterior fossa malformations, hemangioma, arterial anomalies of the cerebral and cervical vasculature, cardiac anomalies including coarctation of the aorta, and eye abnormalities; a sternal cleft or supraumbilical raphe is sometimes included as the “S.” The arterial anomalies are the most consequential, since cerebrovascular abnormalities create a stroke risk that also influences decisions about initiating propranolol. Appropriate screening includes MRI/MRA of the head and neck, echocardiography with imaging of the aortic arch, and ophthalmologic examination.
Lumbosacral ultrasonography (Option B) is the right answer for a different scenario — a segmental hemangioma over the lumbosacral midline, which is associated with LUMBAR/PELVIS syndrome and occult spinal dysraphism. Chromosomal breakage testing (Option A) screens for Fanconi anemia in a child with radial or thumb deficiency. Renal ultrasonography (Option D) is part of a VACTERL workup. A skeletal survey (Option E) has no role here.
Question 10
A 9-year-old boy is evaluated because of asymmetry of the lower extremities. Physical examination shows a geographic capillary stain over the lateral aspect of the right thigh and leg, prominent lateral varicosities, and an increase in both girth and length of the right limb. There is no warmth, thrill, or bruit, and duplex imaging shows no arteriovenous shunting. Which of the following complications is this patient most likely to develop?
A)      Chondrosarcoma
B)      Consumptive coagulopathy with profound thrombocytopenia
C)       High-output cardiac failure
D)      Leg-length discrepancy requiring orthopedic intervention
E)      Pathologic fracture
The correct response is Option D.
Capillary stain, venous varicosities including a persistent embryonic lateral marginal vein, and limb overgrowth in the absence of arteriovenous shunting constitute Klippel-Trénaunay syndrome, a combined capillary-lymphatic-venous malformation associated with somatic PIK3CA mutation. Limb-length discrepancy is the most common clinically significant problem, reported in roughly two thirds of patients, and the affected limb is usually the longer one. Management centers on serial measurement, shoe lifts, and well-timed epiphysiodesis of the contralateral limb when the projected discrepancy exceeds about 2 cm. Compression garments, sclerotherapy or ablation of symptomatic varicosities, and debulking for functional impairment address the vascular component.
High-output cardiac failure (Option C) requires arteriovenous fistulae and characterizes Parkes-Weber syndrome, which duplex imaging excluded here. Chondrosarcoma (Option A) and pathologic fracture (Option E) relate to the enchondromas of Maffucci syndrome and Ollier disease. Question 1
A 46-year-old woman is brought to the emergency department 3 days after undergoing abdominoplasty with flank liposuction. She reports pain out of proportion to her examination findings. Temperature is 102.4°F (39.1°C), heart rate is 128 beats/min, and blood pressure is 88/52 mmHg. Examination shows dusky, poorly demarcated erythema of the lower abdomen and flanks with several tense bullae, crepitus, and thin gray-brown fluid draining from the incision. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A)      Intravenous antibiotic therapy alone
B)      Intravenous antibiotic therapy with local wound care and serial marking of the erythema
C)       Immediate wide surgical debridement with broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy
D)      Bedside incision and drainage with placement of a drain
E)      CT of the abdomen and pelvis before any intervention
The correct response is Option C.
The findings describe necrotizing soft-tissue infection: pain out of proportion to examination, systemic toxicity and hemodynamic instability, indistinct rather than sharply demarcated erythema, bullae, crepitus, and the characteristic thin “dishwater” drainage. This is a surgical emergency. Definitive treatment is immediate, aggressive debridement of all nonviable fascia and subcutaneous tissue with broad-spectrum antibiotic coverage — typically a carbapenem or piperacillin-tazobactam plus vancomycin, with clindamycin added for its antitoxin effect against streptococcal and staphylococcal exotoxins. The wound is left open for a planned second look within 24 hours, since the necrosis at the fascial level is characteristically far more extensive than the cutaneous findings suggest.
Delay is the principal determinant of mortality, which ranges up to 70% overall but falls dramatically with early operative intervention. For that reason, imaging (Option E) should never postpone exploration in a patient who is clinically convincing; CT may show fascial gas or edema but is neither sensitive nor specific enough to rule out the diagnosis. Antibiotics alone (Options A and B) do not penetrate necrotic, thrombosed tissue. Limited bedside drainage (Option D) is inadequate for a process that requires excision of all involved fascia.
Question 2
A 15-year-old girl comes to the office because of a 2-day history of throbbing pain and swelling of the pulp of the right index finger. She recalls no injury. Examination shows a tense, exquisitely tender, erythematous fingertip with fluctuance limited to the pulp space; the flexor sheath is nontender proximal to the distal interphalangeal joint, and passive extension is painless. Which of the following organisms is the most likely cause?
A)      Candida albicans
B)      Eikenella corrodens
C)       Mycobacterium marinum
D)      Pasteurella multocida
E)      Staphylococcus aureus
The correct response is Option E.
This is a felon — a closed-space infection of the fibrous septae of the distal pulp — and Staphylococcus aureus is by far the most common causative organism in felons, paronychias, and hand infections in general. The rigid septae tethering the pulp skin to the distal phalanx periosteum create multiple noncompliant compartments, so pressure rises quickly and the infection can progress to skin necrosis, flexor tenosynovitis, or osteomyelitis of the distal phalanx if not decompressed. Treatment is prompt incision and drainage with disruption of the septae, plus antistaphylococcal antibiotics; local prevalence of methicillin-resistant strains should drive empiric selection. The incision should avoid crossing the distal interphalangeal flexion crease and should preserve the pulp’s sensate distal skin; a longitudinal volar or high lateral incision is standard, and the classic “fish-mouth” incision is avoided because of devascularization and painful scarring.
Eikenella corrodens (Option B) is associated with human bites. Pasteurella multocida (Option D) follows cat and dog bites and characteristically produces rapid-onset cellulitis within 24 hours. Mycobacterium marinum (Option C) causes indolent infections after aquarium or fish-handling exposure. Candida (Option A) is typical of chronic paronychia in patients with repeated wet exposure or diabetes.
Question 3
A 25-year-old man is brought to the emergency department 18 hours after striking another man in the mouth during a fight. Examination shows a 6-mm transverse laceration over the dorsum of the long finger metacarpophalangeal joint with surrounding erythema, swelling, and pain with joint motion. Radiographs show no fracture or retained tooth fragment. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A)      Irrigation in the emergency department, primary closure, and oral cephalexin
B)      Irrigation in the emergency department, wound left open, and oral amoxicillin-clavulanate
C)       Operative exploration, extension of the wound, irrigation and debridement including the joint, wound left open, and intravenous ampicillin-sulbactam
D)      Observation with elevation and splinting, with antibiotics if cellulitis develops
E)      Intravenous vancomycin with delayed primary closure at 48 hours
The correct response is Option C.
Any laceration over the metacarpophalangeal joint sustained in a fight is a human bite — a “fight bite” — until proven otherwise, and it should be assumed to have violated the extensor tendon and joint capsule. The mechanism is deceptive: the injury occurs with the joint flexed, so the tendon and capsular lacerations retract proximally and no longer align with the skin wound when the hand is examined in extension. Delayed presentation with erythema and pain on motion, as here, makes established septic arthritis likely. Management is formal operative exploration with extension of the wound, inspection of the tendon and joint through a full arc of motion, thorough irrigation and debridement, and leaving the wound open, combined with intravenous ampicillin-sulbactam. The organisms are polymicrobial — oral streptococci, Staphylococcus aureus, anaerobes, and Eikenella corrodens, which is resistant to first-generation cephalosporins and clindamycin. Tetanus status should be updated and hepatitis and HIV exposure risk addressed.
Primary closure (Option A) is contraindicated in bite wounds of the hand. Bedside irrigation with oral antibiotics (Option B) is inadequate once the joint is presumed contaminated. Observation (Option D) risks joint destruction and osteomyelitis. Vancomycin alone (Option E) does not cover Eikenella or oral anaerobes, and delayed closure does not substitute for operative debridement.
Question 4
A 34-year-old woman comes to the emergency department 12 hours after her cat bit the dorsum of her right hand. Examination shows two puncture wounds with 4 cm of surrounding erythema, marked tenderness, and early lymphangitic streaking. She is afebrile and has no fluctuance or tenderness along the flexor sheaths. She reports a severe penicillin allergy with prior anaphylaxis. Which of the following is the most appropriate antibiotic therapy?
A)      Cephalexin
B)      Clindamycin
C)       Doxycycline
D)      Metronidazole
E)      Vancomycin
The correct response is Option C.
Cat bites are dominated by Pasteurella multocida, which characteristically produces rapidly progressive cellulitis and lymphangitis within 12 to 24 hours — a much faster tempo than staphylococcal infection. Cat teeth are fine and sharp, inoculating organisms deeply into joints, tendon sheaths, and bone through wounds that appear trivial, which is why cat bites become infected far more often than dog bites. First-line therapy is amoxicillin-clavulanate. In a patient with true penicillin anaphylaxis, doxycycline is an appropriate alternative with reliable Pasteurella activity; a fluoroquinolone or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole combined with an anaerobic agent are other accepted options.
The trap in this question is that the drugs commonly reached for in penicillin-allergic patients with skin and soft-tissue infection — cephalexin (Option A, also cross-reactivity concern), clindamycin (Option B), and vancomycin (Option E) — all have poor or unreliable activity against Pasteurella. Metronidazole (Option D) covers anaerobes only. Puncture wounds should be irrigated and left open; wounds over joints or tendon sheaths, or those with established deep infection, require operative exploration, and rabies and tetanus prophylaxis must be addressed.
Question 5
A 38-year-old man comes to the emergency department because of a 2-day history of increasing pain and swelling of the left ring finger after a splinter injury. Examination shows the finger held in slight flexion, uniform fusiform swelling, tenderness along the volar aspect from the distal palmar crease to the fingertip, and severe pain with passive extension. Temperature is 100.8°F (38.2°C). Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A)      Elevation, splinting, and 24 hours of intravenous antibiotic therapy with reassessment
B)      Aspiration of the flexor sheath for culture followed by oral antibiotic therapy
C)       Operative irrigation of the flexor sheath through proximal and distal counter-incisions with intravenous antibiotic therapy
D)      Open synovectomy with excision of the flexor digitorum superficialis
E)      Ray amputation
The correct response is Option C.
All four Kanavel signs are present — a semiflexed resting posture, fusiform swelling, tenderness along the course of the sheath, and pain with passive extension, which is the most sensitive of the four. Pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis is a closed-space infection in which rising intrasheath pressure compromises the segmental vincular blood supply to the tendon, producing necrosis and adhesions. Standard treatment is urgent operative drainage: a small distal incision near the distal interphalangeal joint and a proximal incision at the A1 pulley, with catheter irrigation of the sheath, combined with intravenous antibiotics, splinting, elevation, and early therapy.
A brief trial of intravenous antibiotics with close reassessment (Option A) is defensible only in a very early presentation with equivocal signs and a reliable setting for hourly reevaluation; this patient has a complete Kanavel tetrad, fever, and a 2-day course. Aspiration with oral antibiotics (Option B) neither decompresses the sheath nor removes purulence. Tendon excision (Option D) and amputation (Option E) are salvage procedures for necrotic tendon or uncontrolled infection, not initial management. Anatomic communication of the small finger and thumb sheaths with the ulnar and radial bursae permits a horseshoe abscess, which should be sought on examination.
Question 6
A 52-year-old woman who underwent implant-based breast reconstruction 5 weeks ago is evaluated because of a 2-week history of progressive erythema and serous drainage around the inframammary incision. She has been afebrile. Cultures from the drainage grow acid-fast bacilli identified as Mycobacterium fortuitum. She has been receiving cephalexin without improvement. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A)      Continue cephalexin and add oral fluconazole
B)      Aspiration of the periprosthetic fluid and a 6-week course of intravenous vancomycin with implant retention
C)       Explantation with capsulectomy and prolonged combination antimycobacterial therapy
D)      Implant exchange with capsulotomy and a 10-day course of oral doxycycline
E)      Observation with local wound care
The correct response is Option C.
Rapidly growing nontuberculous mycobacteria — principally Mycobacterium fortuitum, chelonae, and abscessus — are a classic cause of indolent, culture-negative-on-routine-media periprosthetic infection. The presentation is characteristic: onset weeks to months after surgery rather than in the first postoperative week, an afebrile patient who does not look septic, serous rather than purulent drainage, and failure to respond to standard antistaphylococcal antibiotics. Diagnosis requires that acid-fast smear and mycobacterial culture be specifically requested, since these organisms will not grow on standard cultures held for routine intervals. Because the biofilm on the prosthesis and capsule cannot be sterilized medically, treatment is device removal with capsulectomy plus multidrug antimycobacterial therapy — commonly a macrolide combined with an aminoglycoside, a fluoroquinolone, or another agent based on susceptibilities — for a prolonged course of months, guided by infectious disease consultation. Implant retention strategies (Options B and D) and short antibiotic courses fail. Cephalexin and fluconazole (Option A) do not treat mycobacteria. Observation (Option E) permits progression to skin necrosis and extrusion.
Question 7
A 61-year-old man with type 2 diabetes mellitus and peripheral neuropathy is evaluated because of a 3-month history of a 2-cm ulcer over the plantar aspect of the first metatarsal head. A sterile probe passed into the ulcer contacts hard, gritty bone. Radiographs show cortical irregularity of the metatarsal head. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate is 82 mm/hr. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step to establish the diagnosis and direct therapy?
A)      Superficial wound swab culture
B)      Bone biopsy with histopathologic examination and culture
C)       Three-phase technetium bone scan
D)      Blood cultures
E)      Empiric 6-week course of intravenous vancomycin and piperacillin-tazobactam
The correct response is Option B.
Bone biopsy with culture and histology remains the diagnostic standard for diabetic foot osteomyelitis. It distinguishes osteomyelitis from Charcot neuroarthropathy and identifies the true pathogen with its susceptibilities, which matters because antibiotic therapy typically runs 6 weeks and because empiric regimens frequently miss the organism. A positive probe-to-bone test in this clinical context has a high positive predictive value and, with an erythrocyte sedimentation rate above 70 mm/hr and abnormal plain films, makes osteomyelitis very likely — but “likely” is not a microbiologic diagnosis.
Superficial swab cultures (Option A) sample colonizing flora and correlate poorly with deep bone isolates; they should not be used to select therapy. A three-phase bone scan (Option C) is sensitive but nonspecific and is confounded by neuropathic bone changes; MRI is the imaging study of choice when imaging is needed, particularly for delineating the extent of involvement and abscess. Blood cultures (Option D) are usually negative in chronic contiguous-focus osteomyelitis. Empiric prolonged therapy without a tissue diagnosis (Option E) is a common but avoidable error. Question 8
A 58-year-old woman with poorly controlled type 1 diabetes mellitus is admitted with diabetic ketoacidosis. On hospital day 2 she reports left facial pain and visual changes. Examination shows periorbital edema, proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, and a black eschar on the left middle turbinate and hard palate. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A)      Intravenous vancomycin and piperacillin-tazobactam
B)      Intravenous acyclovir and observation
C)       Intravenous fluconazole with nasal endoscopy in 48 hours
D)      Intravenous liposomal amphotericin B with urgent radical surgical debridement
E)      Hyperbaric oxygen therapy alone
The correct response is Option D.
Necrotic eschar of the palate or turbinates with orbital involvement in a patient with diabetic ketoacidosis is rhino-orbital-cerebral mucormycosis until proven otherwise. The Mucorales are angioinvasive, producing vascular thrombosis and progressive tissue infarction; the black eschar is infarcted tissue, and because the tissue is dead and thrombosed, antifungal drug does not reach the advancing edge. Survival depends on three simultaneous actions: urgent and often disfiguring radical surgical debridement of all necrotic tissue with repeated second looks, systemic liposomal amphotericin B, and aggressive correction of the underlying metabolic derangement — reversal of ketoacidosis and hyperglycemia, and withdrawal of immunosuppression or deferoxamine where applicable. Posaconazole or isavuconazole are used for step-down or salvage therapy. Orbital exenteration is sometimes required. Antibacterial therapy (Option A) and acyclovir (Option B) do not treat the organism. Fluconazole (Option C) has no activity against Mucorales, and any delay for staged endoscopy is dangerous. Hyperbaric oxygen (Option E) is at most an adjunct.

Question 10
A 44-year-old man with a 30-pack-year smoking history and untreated type 2 diabetes mellitus is scheduled to undergo elective abdominal wall reconstruction with mesh. Which of the following preoperative interventions is most likely to reduce his risk for surgical site infection?

B)      Povidone-iodine skin preparation instead of a chlorhexidine-alcohol preparation

E)      A 7-day course of postoperative oral cephalexin
The correct response is Option C.
Decolonization with intranasal mupirocin plus chlorhexidine bathing in the days before surgery is among the best-supported interventions for reducing surgical site infection. A large randomized trial demonstrated roughly a 60% reduction in Staphylococcus aureus surgical site infections among nasal carriers treated with this protocol, and meta-analyses have shown reductions in overall infection rates on the order of 40 to 45%.

Congenital and Pediatric Hand
Question 1
A 2-year-old boy is brought to the office for evaluation of a small right thumb. Physical examination shows a flat thenar eminence, absence of the flexor pollicis longus flexion crease, a lax ulnar collateral ligament at the metacarpophalangeal joint, and a narrow first web space. When asked to pick up a raisin, he consistently uses a scissor pinch between the index and long fingers and ignores the thumb. Radiographs show a tapered, underdeveloped first metacarpal base. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A)      Observation and occupational therapy with reassessment at age 5 years
B)      First web space release with ulnar collateral ligament reconstruction alone
C)       Flexor digitorum superficialis opponensplasty with ulnar collateral ligament reconstruction and web release
D)      Ablation of the thumb with index finger pollicization
E)      Free vascularized second toe transfer
The correct response is Option D.
The single determinant that separates a Blauth type IIIA from a type IIIB thumb is the presence or absence of a stable carpometacarpal joint, and the tapered, underdeveloped metacarpal base on radiograph is the radiographic hallmark of type IIIB. The clinical corollary is equally informative: a child who bypasses the thumb in favor of scissor pinch between the index and long fingers has no cortical representation of the thumb and does not use it. Types IIIB, IV, and V are treated by ablation of the deficient thumb and pollicization of the index finger.
Reconstructive procedures directed at the existing thumb (Options B and C) are appropriate for types II and IIIA, where the carpometacarpal joint is stable; typically opponensplasty (Huber abductor digiti minimi transfer or a flexor digitorum superficialis transfer), ulnar collateral ligament stabilization, and first web deepening are combined. No amount of tendon transfer will make a child use a thumb with an unstable or absent basilar joint. Delaying to age 5 (Option A) allows the scissor-pinch pattern to entrench and makes both the child and the family more resistant to thumb ablation. Toe transfer (Option E) is not the standard reconstruction for congenital thumb hypoplasia in this setting.
Question 2
A 10-month-old girl with a type IV (pouce flottant) thumb deficiency is scheduled to undergo index finger pollicization. During the procedure, which of the following structures is used to create the new carpometacarpal joint of the reconstructed thumb?
A)      The index carpometacarpal joint after transposition of the entire metacarpal
B)      The index metacarpophalangeal joint after resection of most of the index metacarpal shaft
C)       The index proximal interphalangeal joint
D)      The trapeziometacarpal joint of the residual thumb ray
E)      A costochondral graft interposed at the trapezium
The correct response is Option B.
In pollicization, the index finger is transferred on its neurovascular pedicle after the great majority of the index metacarpal shaft is resected, leaving the metacarpal head, which is then secured in hyperextension to the metacarpal base or trapezium. The retained index metacarpophalangeal joint becomes the new thumb carpometacarpal joint, and hyperextending and fixing the metacarpal head in that position is what prevents subsequent hyperextension deformity of the new basilar joint. The index proximal interphalangeal joint becomes the new metacarpophalangeal joint, and the index distal interphalangeal joint becomes the new interphalangeal joint. Osteosynthesis is therefore between the index proximal phalanx and the retained metacarpal head or metacarpal base.
The digit must also be shortened, pronated approximately 100 to 160 degrees, and positioned in about 40 degrees of palmar abduction so that pulp-to-pulp pinch with the long and ring fingers is possible. Failure to pronate and abduct adequately is the most common technical cause of a functionally poor result. Option A would leave the digit far too long and in the plane of the other fingers. Options C, D, and E do not describe the procedure.
Question 3
During index finger pollicization, the intrinsic musculature of the transferred digit is preserved and reassigned to power the reconstructed thumb. The first dorsal interosseous muscle assumes which of the following new functions?
A)      Abductor pollicis brevis
B)      Abductor pollicis longus
C)       Adductor pollicis
D)      Extensor pollicis brevis
E)      Flexor pollicis longus
The correct response is Option A.
The intrinsic muscles are rebalanced in a predictable pattern that is worth memorizing as a set. The first dorsal interosseous becomes the abductor pollicis brevis, and the first palmar interosseous becomes the adductor pollicis— the two intrinsics simply retain their vectors relative to the newly positioned digit. On the extrinsic side, the extensor digitorum communis of the index is shortened to act as the abductor pollicis longus, and the extensor indicis proprius becomes the extensor pollicis longus. The flexor tendons are generally not disturbed; they accommodate to the shortened skeleton over time, and formal shortening risks adhesion and loss of excursion.
The most common error in answering this class of question is transposing the two interossei. A useful anchor: the dorsal interosseous abducts a finger and therefore abducts the new thumb (abductor pollicis brevis), while the palmar interosseous adducts and therefore becomes the adductor pollicis. Options B and D describe extrinsic tendon reassignments, and the flexor pollicis longus (Option E) has no intrinsic muscle equivalent.
Question 4
A full-term male newborn has an isolated type IV thumb deficiency with no other anomalies identified on initial examination. His parents ask when reconstruction should be performed. Which of the following is the most appropriate timing for pollicization?
A)      1 to 2 months of age
B)      6 to 12 months of age
C)       3 years of age
D)      6 years of age
E)      After skeletal maturity
The correct response is Option B.
Pollicization is most commonly performed between 6 and 12 months of age, and successful results have been reported across a window of roughly 3 months to 3 years. The rationale is developmental rather than technical. A child begins to incorporate the thumb into grasp patterns after about 3 months of age, and pinch and prehension patterns consolidate over the first few years. Operating within the first year allows the pollicized digit to be integrated naturally as the child’s manipulative repertoire develops, without the need to unlearn an established scissor-pinch pattern.
Operating before 3 months (Option A) offers no developmental advantage while adding the risks of immature cardiopulmonary physiology and the technical difficulty of very small vessels. Delaying to preschool or school age (Options C, D, and E) makes retraining harder and, practically, allows family ambivalence about thumb ablation to harden. Before proceeding, an evaluation for associated conditions is mandatory, since thumb hypoplasia is associated with VACTERL association, Holt-Oram syndrome, thrombocytopenia-absent radius syndrome, and Fanconi anemia. That workup includes renal ultrasonography, echocardiography, spine radiographs, complete blood count, and chromosomal breakage testing — the last of which can identify Fanconi anemia before marrow failure develops.
Question 5
A 14-month-old boy is evaluated because of a small right thumb. Physical examination shows hypoplasia of the thenar musculature with weak opposition, a narrow first web space, and mild laxity of the metacarpophalangeal joint ulnar collateral ligament. A)      Continued observation
B)      Opponensplasty with first web space release and ulnar collateral ligament stabilization

The correct response is Option B.
This is a Blauth type II or IIIA thumb: the deficiency involves thenar muscles, the first web space, and the ulnar collateral ligament, but the carpometacarpal joint is stable and the child uses the thumb. Question 6
A 5-day-old newborn is evaluated because of a markedly shortened right forearm with the hand deviated radially and a completely absent thumb. Radiographs show complete absence of the radius. Which of the following studies is most important in the initial evaluation of this infant?
A)      Serum uric acid concentration
B)      Chromosomal breakage testing and complete blood count
C)       Nerve conduction studies of the upper extremity
D)      Bone age radiographs
E)      MRI of the cervical spine
The correct response is Option B.
Radial longitudinal deficiency mandates a systemic workup, because the limb anomaly is frequently a marker for a syndrome with far greater consequences than the hand itself. Fanconi anemia is the most important to exclude: it is autosomal recessive, causes progressive marrow failure typically presenting between ages 5 and 10 years, predisposes to leukemia and solid tumors, and is diagnosed by a chromosomal breakage test using diepoxybutane or mitomycin C. Because the hematologic manifestations appear years after the limb anomaly, the diagnosis will be missed unless it is specifically sought in infancy — and it is potentially curable with hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. A complete blood count screens for thrombocytopenia-absent radius syndrome, in which thrombocytopenia is present in infancy and improves with age, and in which — distinctively — the thumb is always present despite an absent radius.
The remaining associations round out the evaluation: VACTERL association (renal ultrasonography, echocardiography, spine radiographs, assessment for anal atresia and tracheoesophageal fistula) and Holt-Oram syndrome (cardiac septal defects and conduction abnormalities, TBX5 mutation). The other listed studies (Options A, C, D, and E) have no established role in this initial screening.
Question 7
An 18-month-old girl is brought to the office because of complete syndactyly involving the ring-small web and the long-ring web of the left hand, with fusion extending to the fingertips. Radiographs show separate distal phalanges without bony fusion. Which of the following is the most appropriate operative plan?
A)      Simultaneous release of both webs at a single operation with local flaps and no skin grafts
B)      Release of the ring-small web first, with a dorsal flap for web reconstruction and full-thickness skin grafts as needed
C)       Release of the long-ring web first, with linear closure alone
D)      Delay of all surgery until the child is 6 years of age
E)      Release of both webs with amputation of the small finger
The correct response is Option B.
Two principles govern this case. First, border digit syndactyly is released first and earliest — thumb-index and ring-small — because the adjacent digits differ most in length and in the arc of motion at each joint, so unreleased fusion causes progressive angular deformity and joint contracture of the shorter digit. Second, adjacent webs are not released at the same operation, because each digit receives skin flaps and grafts from both sides simultaneously and the risk of vascular compromise to the intervening digit is unacceptable; the second web is released after an interval of roughly 3 to 6 months.
Web space reconstruction uses a broad dorsal advancement flap to recreate the normal dorsal-to-volar slope of the commissure, with interdigitating zigzag flaps along the digits. Because the circumference of two conjoined digits is less than that of two separate digits, primary closure of the entire surface is not possible in a complete syndactyly, and full-thickness skin grafts — usually from the groin crease or hypothenar eminence — are required. Techniques that avoid grafting exist but risk web creep and scar contracture in complete cases. Linear closure alone (Option C) invites contracture. Delay to school age (Option D) permits fixed deformity. Amputation (Option E) is not indicated with separate skeletal elements.
Question 8
A 2-year-old girl is brought to the office because of a duplicated right thumb. Examination shows two thumbs of nearly equal size diverging at the metacarpophalangeal joint. Radiographs show a single, broadened first metacarpal with a bifid head articulating with two separate proximal phalanges. Which of the following is the most likely Wassel classification of this duplication?
A)      Type I
B)      Type II
C)       Type III
D)      Type IV
E)      Type VII
The correct response is Option D.
The Wassel classification counts the level of skeletal duplication from distal to proximal, with odd numbers indicating incomplete (bifid) and even numbers complete duplication: type I, bifid distal phalanx; type II, duplicated distal phalanx; type III, bifid proximal phalanx; type IV, duplicated proximal phalanx; type V, bifid metacarpal; type VI, duplicated metacarpal; type VII, triphalangeal thumb. A single broadened metacarpal with two complete proximal phalanges articulating at the metacarpophalangeal joint is a type IV, which is the most common variant, accounting for roughly 40 to 45% of cases.
Treatment is not simple ablation of the smaller thumb. The dominant thumb is retained and reconstructed by transferring the ulnar collateral ligament with a periosteal sleeve from the ablated digit, rebalancing the intrinsic and extrinsic tendons, which frequently insert eccentrically and cause postoperative angulation, and performing a corrective metacarpal head osteotomy where the articular surface is skewed. Failure to address ligament stability and tendon centralization produces the classic late zigzag deformity. Preaxial polydactyly is usually unilateral and sporadic, whereas a triphalangeal thumb or postaxial polydactyly is more often inherited and syndrome-associated.
Question 9
A 14-month-old boy is brought to the office because his right thumb has been held in a flexed position at the interphalangeal joint for several months. Examination shows an interphalangeal joint that cannot be passively extended fully and a palpable firm nodule over the volar aspect of the metacarpophalangeal joint. There is no history of triggering or catching. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A)      Observation, with release considered if the contracture persists
B)      Immediate percutaneous release of the A1 pulley
C)       Corticosteroid injection into the flexor sheath
D)      Serial casting followed by tendon lengthening
E)      Excision of the flexor digitorum superficialis slip
The correct response is Option A.
Congenital — more accurately, pediatric — trigger thumb typically presents as a fixed flexion posture of the interphalangeal joint with a palpable Notta node at the A1 pulley, rather than the intermittent triggering seen in adults; true triggering is uncommon in children. A substantial proportion resolve spontaneously, with reported rates varying widely across series but generally supporting an initial period of observation with or without splinting in children under about 1 to 2 years of age. Persistent contracture beyond that window is treated with open release of the A1 pulley, which is highly successful.
Percutaneous release (Option B) is avoided in children because the radial digital nerve to the thumb crosses obliquely and superficially near the A1 pulley and is at high risk with a blind technique. Steroid injection (Option C) is not standard in pediatric trigger thumb. Tendon lengthening (Option E) is unnecessary — the pathology is a size mismatch between the nodular flexor pollicis longus and the pulley, corrected by releasing the pulley. Bilateral involvement occurs in about a quarter of children, so the contralateral thumb should always be examined.
Question 10
A term newborn has a deep circumferential soft-tissue groove around the mid portion of the left index finger. The digit distal to the groove is markedly edematous, dusky, and cool, with sluggish capillary refill. Two other digits have shallow, uncomplicated grooves, and the long and ring fingers are fused distally with a proximal sinus tract between them. Which of the following is the most appropriate management of the index finger?
A)      Observation with serial examination and elevation
B)      Amputation of the digit distal to the groove
C)       Urgent circumferential release of the constriction band with Z-plasties
D)      Application of a compressive wrap and reevaluation at 6 months
E)      Elective release at 6 to 12 months of age
The correct response is Option C.
Amniotic constriction band sequence produces a spectrum ranging from a shallow, cosmetically insignificant groove to intrauterine amputation, and the distal fusion with a proximal sinus tract described in the long and ring fingers is acrosyndactyly, a characteristic associated finding that distinguishes this condition from simple syndactyly. Most bands can be released electively in the first year of life (Option E), and staged release of opposite halves of the circumference has traditionally been advocated to protect the residual circulation, although single-stage circumferential release is well described and widely used.
The exception — and the point of this question — is a band causing acute distal vascular or lymphatic compromise, which is a surgical emergency. A dusky, cool digit with sluggish refill requires urgent release of the constricting ring with excision of the fibrous band and Z-plasty or W-plasty reconstruction of the contour to prevent recurrent circumferential scar. Observation or compressive dressings (Options A and D) risk progression to necrosis and autoamputation. Amputation (Option B) is not indicated when the distal part is salvageable by decompression. Notably, the condition is sporadic and not associated with a systemic syndrome, so a broad genetic workup is generally unnecessary.
Facial Fractures
Question 1
A 32-year-old man undergoes open reduction and internal fixation of a displaced left zygomaticomaxillary complex fracture through upper eyelid, transconjunctival, and intraoral approaches. The infraorbital rim and the zygomaticomaxillary buttress appear well aligned, but the surgeon is concerned about residual malar rotation. Which of the following anatomic landmarks is most reliable for confirming three-dimensional reduction of the zygoma?
A)      The zygomatic arch
B)      The zygomaticofrontal suture
C)       The zygomaticomaxillary buttress
D)      The zygomaticosphenoid suture along the lateral orbital wall
E)      The infraorbital rim
The correct response is Option D.
The zygomaticosphenoid suture on the lateral orbital wall is the single most reliable indicator of anatomic reduction of the zygomaticomaxillary complex. Its value comes from geometry: it is a broad, flat, obliquely oriented interface that is sensitive to rotation of the zygoma about all three axes. A malar segment can be rotated axially and still look acceptable at the rim and the buttress, which are narrow, curved, or oblique interfaces that tolerate malrotation without an obvious step-off, particularly when comminuted or obscured by edema. Alignment of the greater wing of the sphenoid with the posterior surface of the zygoma along its full length confirms that rotation has been corrected, which in turn protects orbital volume and malar projection.
This is why exposure of the lateral orbital wall through an upper blepharoplasty or lateral brow incision is routinely included even when the rim and buttress are already exposed. The zygomaticofrontal suture (Option B) is a single point and permits rotation about it. The arch (Option A) is relevant mainly to projection and facial width and is often not directly exposed. The rim and buttress (Options C and E) are useful for fixation but are poor arbiters of rotational accuracy. A related trap: if the rim appears reduced but the zygoma clearly is not, suspect an unreduced ipsilateral naso-orbito-ethmoid fracture, which must be reduced first.
Question 2
A 28-year-old woman has a displaced right zygomaticomaxillary complex fracture with malar flattening and a 2-mm step-off at the infraorbital rim. Intraoperative examination after reduction shows a stable, well-aligned zygomaticosphenoid suture and an orbital floor defect measuring less than 1 cm2 without herniation of orbital contents. Which of the following combinations of sites is most appropriate for internal fixation?
A)      Zygomaticofrontal suture, zygomaticomaxillary buttress, and infraorbital rim
B)      Zygomaticofrontal suture, zygomaticomaxillary buttress, and orbital floor
C)       Zygomaticofrontal suture, nasomaxillary buttress, and infraorbital rim
D)      Zygomatic arch, orbital floor, and alveolus
E)      Zygomaticomaxillary buttress, nasomaxillary buttress, and infraorbital rim
The correct response is Option A.
The zygoma articulates with the frontal bone, the maxilla, the sphenoid, and the temporal bone. For a displaced but reducible fracture, three-point fixation at the zygomaticofrontal suture, the zygomaticomaxillary buttress, and the infraorbital rim provides stable three-dimensional control. The buttress is the thickest bone and best resists masseteric pull; the zygomaticofrontal suture controls vertical position; the rim controls the anterior projection and contour that the patient will see. Two-point fixation is adequate for many minimally displaced patterns, while highly comminuted fractures may require four-point fixation including the arch to restore facial width and projection.
The orbital floor (Option B) is reconstructed rather than plated, and only when indicated — generally a defect exceeding roughly 2 cm2 or half the floor, or clinically significant herniation, entrapment, or enophthalmos. A defect under 1 cm2 without herniation, as here, does not require reconstruction. The nasomaxillary buttress (Options C and E) is a legitimate vertical facial buttress, but the zygoma does not articulate with the nasal bones, so it plays no part in zygomatic fixation. The alveolus (Option D) is not a zygomatic articulation.
Question 3
A 35-year-old man sustains a midface injury in a motor vehicle collision. Examination shows a flattened, widened nasal dorsum, an intercanthal distance of 42 mm, bilateral periorbital ecchymoses, and rounding of the medial canthal angles. Bimanual examination with an instrument in the nose and a finger over the medial canthus demonstrates mobility of the central fragment, which bears the medial canthal tendon as a single unit. Which of the following is the most appropriate management of the medial canthal region?
A)      Reduction and plate fixation of the central fragment without canthal detachment
B)      Detachment and transnasal wiring of the medial canthal tendon anterior and inferior to the lacrimal fossa
C)       Detachment and transnasal wiring of the medial canthal tendon posterior and superior to the lacrimal fossa
D)      Lateral canthopexy with observation of the medial canthus
E)      Dacryocystorhinostomy at the time of fracture repair
The correct response is Option A.
The critical first determination in a naso-orbito-ethmoid fracture is whether the medial canthal tendon remains attached to a single, large, manipulable central fragment (Markowitz-Manson type I), is attached to a comminuted central fragment (type II), or is avulsed from bone (type III). When the tendon remains attached to a single large fragment, as demonstrated here by bimanual examination, the correct treatment is anatomic reduction and rigid fixation of that fragment with the tendon left undisturbed. Detaching an intact canthal insertion converts a straightforward problem into a difficult one, since no reattachment technique reproduces the native insertion.
When transnasal canthopexy is required — types II and III — the wire must be passed posterior and superior to the lacrimal fossa (Option C describes the correct vector but not the correct indication for this patient). Placing the wire anteriorly or inferiorly, the intuitive but wrong direction, produces persistent rounding of the canthus and residual telecanthus, because the native insertion of the posterior limb lies on the posterior lacrimal crest. Overcorrection is advised, since some relapse is expected. Lateral canthopexy (Option D) does not address medial telecanthus. Dacryocystorhinostomy (Option E) is reserved for established, symptomatic epiphora documented after the fracture has healed; acute lacrimal exploration is not routine.
Question 4
A 9-year-old boy is brought to the emergency department after being struck in the left eye with a baseball. He has minimal periorbital ecchymosis or swelling. He is nauseated, has vomited twice, and has a heart rate of 52 beats/min. Examination shows marked limitation of upgaze on the left with pain on attempted elevation. CT shows a small linear defect of the left orbital floor with a soft-tissue density extending through it. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A)      Observation with reassessment in 2 weeks
B)      Nasal decongestants, corticosteroids, and reevaluation in 7 to 10 days
C)       Urgent operative exploration and release of the entrapped tissue with floor reconstruction
D)      Forced duction testing in 2 weeks, with surgery if positive
E)      Ophthalmologic referral for prism correction
The correct response is Option C.
This is a white-eyed blowout fracture, and it is a surgical emergency in the pediatric population. The pediatric orbital floor is greensticked rather than comminuted, so it springs shut around herniated tissue like a trapdoor, incarcerating the inferior rectus muscle and its surrounding tissue. The deceptive feature is the paucity of external findings — minimal ecchymosis or edema, hence “white-eyed” — in a child with dramatic motility restriction. The associated nausea, vomiting, and bradycardia represent the oculocardiac reflex, mediated through the trigeminal and vagal pathways, and its presence signals true incarceration.
Entrapped muscle becomes ischemic; delay produces permanent fibrosis and lasting diplopia. Release should be accomplished within 24 to 48 hours, and the sooner the better. This is a specific exception to the general adult algorithm — observation for 1 to 2 weeks to allow edema to resolve (Options A, B, and D), with repair for a defect greater than roughly half the floor or 2 cm2, enophthalmos exceeding 2 mm, or persistent symptomatic diplopia in functional gaze. Any child with restricted motility and minimal swelling after periorbital trauma should be assumed to have entrapment until proven otherwise.
Question 5
A 24-year-old man has an isolated, minimally comminuted, displaced fracture of the left mandibular angle without other facial fractures. He is dentate with a stable occlusion after reduction. Which of the following approaches to fixation is most consistent with the Champy technique?
A)      A single miniplate along the superior border of the mandible via a transoral approach
B)      Two 2.4-mm locking reconstruction plates along the inferior border via a transcervical approach
C)       A single lag screw across the fracture through a transcervical approach
D)      Closed reduction with 6 weeks of maxillomandibular fixation
E)      Two miniplates, one at the superior border and one at the inferior border, via a transcervical approach
The correct response is Option A.
Champy demonstrated that the mandible experiences tension along its superior (alveolar) border and compression along its inferior border during function, and that a single monocortical miniplate placed along the “ideal line of osteosynthesis” — for the angle, along the external oblique ridge — neutralizes those tensile forces adequately in a simple angle fracture. The plate is placed transorally, monocortical screws avoid the inferior alveolar nerve and tooth roots, and the technique permits functionally stable fixation without maxillomandibular fixation. Its advantages are a shorter operation, no external scar, and no risk to the marginal mandibular nerve.
Load-bearing reconstruction plates (Option B) are reserved for comminuted, atrophic, or infected mandibles and defect situations, where the hardware must carry the entire functional load. Two miniplates (Option E) are used by some surgeons for angle fractures but represent the alternative “load-sharing with two-point fixation” philosophy and require an external or combined approach for the inferior plate; the Champy technique is specifically the single superior-border plate. A lag screw (Option C) is applicable to oblique fractures, particularly of the symphysis and body. Closed treatment with maxillomandibular fixation (Option D) remains acceptable in selected patients but is not the Champy technique and carries nutritional and airway drawbacks.
Question 6
A 40-year-old woman sustains a unilateral fracture of the mandibular condylar neck with 3 mm of vertical ramus shortening and a mildly displaced condylar head that remains within the glenoid fossa. Occlusion is reproducible with guiding elastics, and mouth opening is 32 mm. She has no other facial fractures. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A)      Open reduction and internal fixation via a retromandibular approach
B)      Condylectomy with costochondral grafting
C)       Closed treatment with a short period of maxillomandibular fixation, guiding elastics, and early functional therapy
D)      Rigid maxillomandibular fixation for 8 weeks
E)      Endoscopic-assisted open reduction with two-plate fixation
The correct response is Option C.
Most condylar fractures in adults with restorable occlusion are managed nonoperatively with a brief period of maxillomandibular fixation or guiding elastics followed by early mobilization and functional therapy. The condyle remodels well, and the priorities are a stable occlusion and a functional range of motion rather than radiographic perfection. Prolonged rigid fixation (Option D) risks ankylosis, particularly in children, and is avoided.
Open treatment is indicated for the recognized absolute and relative criteria: displacement of the condylar head out of the glenoid fossa, lateral extracapsular displacement, inability to obtain adequate occlusion by closed means, a foreign body in the joint, bilateral condylar fractures with associated midface fractures where vertical height must be restored, and significant ramus shortening — commonly cited thresholds are angulation greater than 30 to 45 degrees or shortening greater than 4 to 5 mm. This patient meets none of these. Approaches for open treatment include retromandibular, preauricular, submandibular, and endoscopic-assisted transoral techniques (Options A and E), each carrying risk to the facial nerve. Condylectomy with costochondral grafting (Option B) is a reconstructive procedure for ankylosis or condylar loss, not acute fracture care.
Question 7
A 30-year-old man sustains a frontal sinus injury. CT shows a comminuted, displaced anterior table fracture, a comminuted posterior table fracture with 6 mm of displacement, pneumocephalus, and obstruction of both nasofrontal outflow tracts. He has clear rhinorrhea that tests positive for beta-2 transferrin. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A)      Observation with head elevation and serial imaging
B)      Open reduction of the anterior table alone
C)       Frontal sinus obliteration with autologous fat after removal of all mucosa
D)      Cranialization of the frontal sinus with removal of the posterior table and all mucosa, plugging of the nasofrontal ducts, and pericranial flap coverage
E)      Endoscopic sinus surgery to restore nasofrontal outflow
The correct response is Option D.
The two variables that drive frontal sinus management are the posterior table and the nasofrontal outflow tract. A significantly comminuted and displaced posterior table with a persistent cerebrospinal fluid leak means the dural barrier is disrupted and the sinus can no longer be safely separated from the cranial cavity. Cranialization — removal of the remaining posterior table, meticulous removal of all sinus mucosa including burring of the inner cortex to eliminate the invaginations of Breschet, obliteration of the nasofrontal ducts, and interposition of a vascularized pericranial flap between the nasal cavity and the intracranial space — allows the frontal lobes to expand into the space and eliminates the sinus. The anterior table is then reduced and plated for contour.
Obliteration (Option C) is appropriate when the posterior table is intact or minimally displaced but the outflow tract is irreparably obstructed. Isolated anterior table repair (Option B) is correct only when the posterior table is intact and the outflow tract is patent. Observation (Option A) risks meningitis, mucocele, and mucopyocele. Endoscopic approaches (Option E) are increasingly used to preserve outflow in selected fractures but not with a comminuted, displaced posterior table and an active leak. Late complications of inadequately treated frontal sinus injury — mucocele, osteomyelitis, brain abscess — may present years afterward, so long-term follow-up is essential.
Question 8
A 47-year-old woman sustains midface trauma. Examination shows mobility of the entire midface including the nasal complex and both zygomata, with the frontal bone stable; there is malocclusion with an anterior open bite and cerebrospinal fluid rhinorrhea. CT shows fractures through both zygomatic arches, the lateral orbital walls, the orbital floors, the nasofrontal region, and the pterygoid plates. Which of the following best classifies this injury?
A)      Le Fort I
B)      Le Fort II
C)       Le Fort III
D)      Le Fort I on the right and Le Fort II on the left
E)      Isolated bilateral zygomaticomaxillary complex fractures
The correct response is Option C.
A Le Fort III injury is complete craniofacial disjunction: the fracture passes through the nasofrontal suture, the medial orbital walls, the orbital floors, the lateral orbital walls at the zygomaticofrontal sutures, and the zygomatic arches, separating the entire midface from the cranial base. The involvement of both arches and the zygomaticofrontal region — such that the zygomata move with the midface — is what distinguishes it from Le Fort II, in which the zygomata remain attached to the cranium and the fracture takes a pyramidal course across the nasofrontal region, medial orbits, and orbital rims to the lateral maxillary walls. Le Fort I is a transverse fracture separating the maxillary alveolus and palate from the upper midface.
The unifying feature worth remembering is that all Le Fort patterns involve the pterygoid plates — that is the common denominator on CT. In practice, pure Le Fort patterns are uncommon, and different levels frequently coexist on the two sides. Repair proceeds by reestablishing occlusion with maxillomandibular fixation and then reconstructing the vertical and horizontal buttresses from stable cranial bone downward, restoring facial height, width, and projection. Cerebrospinal fluid rhinorrhea in this setting usually resolves with reduction and observation.
Question 9
A 7-year-old girl falls from a playground structure. She has pain at the mandibular symphysis and mild malocclusion. CT shows a nondisplaced greenstick fracture of the symphysis and a minimally displaced right condylar neck fracture. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A)      Open reduction and internal fixation of both fractures with 2.0-mm plates
B)      A soft diet with analgesia and close observation, with brief guiding elastics if occlusion drifts
C)       Rigid maxillomandibular fixation for 6 weeks
D)      Open reduction of the symphysis with resorbable plates and 4 weeks of maxillomandibular fixation
E)      External fixation
The correct response is Option B.
Pediatric mandible fractures differ from adult fractures in three ways that all favor conservative treatment. First, the child’s mandible is elastic and rich in cancellous bone, so fractures are frequently greenstick and minimally displaced, and healing is rapid — often clinically united within 3 to 4 weeks. Second, the mandible contains developing tooth buds throughout the body and symphysis, and screws placed in these regions can injure them; when fixation is unavoidable, monocortical screws are placed at the inferior border below the tooth buds, and resorbable systems are often preferred. Third, prolonged immobilization causes ankylosis and growth disturbance far more readily than in adults, so periods of maxillomandibular fixation are kept short — on the order of 2 to 3 weeks when used at all — and early mobilization is favored.
Condylar fractures in children are managed nonoperatively in nearly all circumstances, with close follow-up for growth disturbance and facial asymmetry, since the condyle is a growth center. Options A, C, and D over-treat a minimally displaced injury and introduce risk to tooth buds and to the temporomandibular joint. External fixation (Option E) has no role here. Any child treated for a mandible fracture should be followed longitudinally through skeletal maturity.
Question 10
A 26-year-old man is evaluated 3 days after being struck in the nose. Examination shows deviation of the nasal dorsum to the left, mild airway obstruction, and resolving edema. The septum is midline without hematoma, and there is no cerebrospinal fluid rhinorrhea. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A)      Immediate open septorhinoplasty
B)      Closed reduction under local or general anesthesia within the next several days
C)       Observation with reassessment at 3 months, followed by septorhinoplasty if needed
D)      Nasal packing alone for 5 days
E)      CT of the facial skeleton before any intervention
The correct response is Option B.
Closed reduction of a displaced nasal fracture is best performed either within the first few hours before edema develops or, more practically, after edema subsides — generally days 3 to 7 in adults and days 3 to 5 in children, and before roughly 2 weeks, when early bony union makes closed manipulation ineffective. This patient is squarely within that window. Reduction is followed by intranasal splinting or packing and an external splint for approximately 7 to 10 days.
Waiting 3 months (Option C) forfeits the opportunity for closed reduction and commits the patient to a formal osteotomy-based operation. Immediate open septorhinoplasty (Option A) is not standard for an acute isolated nasal fracture. Packing alone (Option D) does not reduce anything. Facial Paralysis
Question 1
A 52-year-old man undergoes resection of a malignant parotid tumor with sacrifice of the facial nerve from the stylomastoid foramen to the distal branches. The distal branches and facial musculature are intact and viable. He requires postoperative radiation therapy beginning in 6 weeks. Which of the following reconstructive options is most likely to provide the earliest return of midface tone and movement?
A)      Cross-facial nerve grafting alone
B)      Free gracilis transfer innervated by a cross-facial nerve graft
C)       Masseteric (nerve to masseter) nerve transfer to the distal facial nerve branches at the time of resection
D)      Static suspension with fascia lata
E)      Temporalis tendon transfer
The correct response is Option C.
When viable distal facial nerve branches and non-atrophied facial musculature are available, a regional donor nerve transfer provides the fastest reinnervation because the donor axons are immediately adjacent to the target. The nerve to masseter, a branch of the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve, is found roughly 3 cm anterior to the tragus and 1 cm inferior to the zygomatic arch, deep to the masseter; it carries a high axonal load, is close to the recipient, and typically produces movement within 3 to 6 months. Its trade-off is that the resulting smile is initially volitional, driven by biting, though many patients develop spontaneity with training.
Cross-facial nerve grafting alone (Option A) requires axonal regeneration across a long sural graft from the contralateral face at roughly 1 mm per day, delaying reinnervation by 9 to 12 months and delivering a diluted axonal load; it produces the most spontaneous result and is often combined with masseteric transfer in a “babysitter” strategy. Free muscle transfer (Option B) is reserved for cases where the native musculature is no longer viable. Static suspension (Option D) and temporalis tendon transfer (Option E) provide symmetry and volitional excursion but no reinnervation of native muscle. Time from denervation is the governing variable: beyond roughly 18 to 24 months, motor endplates degenerate and nerve-only strategies fail.
Question 2
A 26-year-old woman has complete flaccid facial paralysis of the left face 4 years after resection of an acoustic neuroma. Electromyography of the left zygomaticus major shows no motor unit potentials and dense fibrillations. She desires restoration of a spontaneous smile and is willing to undergo staged procedures. Which of the following is the most appropriate reconstruction?
A)      Direct repair of the facial nerve
B)      Interposition cable nerve grafting from the facial nerve stump
C)       Hypoglossal-to-facial nerve transfer
D)      Cross-facial nerve grafting followed 9 to 12 months later by free gracilis transfer
E)      Isolated masseteric-to-facial nerve transfer
The correct response is Option D.
Four years of denervation means the motor endplates of the native mimetic muscles have degenerated irreversibly, which the electromyographic findings confirm. Once that has occurred, no strategy that simply delivers axons to the native muscle — direct repair, cable grafting, or nerve transfer to the facial nerve (Options A, B, C, and E) — can restore movement, because there is no functioning muscle to reinnervate. The reconstruction must therefore import new muscle.
The two-stage cross-facial nerve graft and free gracilis transfer remains the reference standard when spontaneity is the priority. In stage one, a sural nerve graft is coapted to a redundant buccal or zygomatic branch on the normal side (selected so that its sacrifice does not weaken the normal smile) and tunneled across the upper lip; regeneration is monitored by an advancing Tinel sign over 9 to 12 months. In stage two, a gracilis muscle is transferred, revascularized, and its obturator nerve coapted to the matured graft. Because the drive comes from the contralateral facial nerve, the resulting smile can be spontaneous and emotionally coupled. The trade-offs are the long interval and a somewhat weaker excursion than a masseteric-driven single-stage gracilis, which is the main alternative and is often chosen when a faster, stronger result matters more than spontaneity.
Question 3
A 68-year-old man with long-standing flaccid facial paralysis and multiple medical comorbidities wants improvement in oral competence and smile with a single operation and the shortest possible recovery. He is not a candidate for prolonged anesthesia or staged microsurgery. Which of the following is the most appropriate reconstruction?
A)      Cross-facial nerve grafting
B)      Free gracilis transfer with masseteric innervation
C)       Lengthening temporalis myoplasty (temporalis tendon transfer to the oral commissure)
D)      Hypoglossal-to-facial nerve transfer
E)      Observation with oral commissure taping
The correct response is Option C.
Temporalis tendon transfer is the workhorse single-stage dynamic option for patients who cannot undergo free tissue transfer. In the orthodromic lengthening myoplasty, the coronoid process is osteotomized and the temporalis tendon is mobilized and advanced to the modiolus and upper lip, either directly or with a tendon graft; the muscle retains its native origin and its innervation by the trigeminal nerve, so no nerve regeneration is required and movement is available essentially immediately with rehabilitation. Recovery is faster, operative time shorter, and the physiologic burden much lower than for a free muscle transfer.
Its limitations are that the smile is trigeminally driven and initiated by biting, and that excursion, while functional, is less than a well-executed gracilis transfer. Because the smile vector is fixed by the tendon inset, careful attention to vector and tension is essential.
The nerve-based options (Options A and D) require intact motor endplates, which long-standing paralysis has destroyed, and require months of regeneration. Free gracilis transfer (Option B) is excluded by the patient’s comorbidities and preferences. Observation (Option E) does not address oral incompetence. Static suspension with fascia lata or acellular dermal matrix is a reasonable adjunct or alternative when even temporalis transfer is too much.
Question 4
A 43-year-old woman comes to the office 2 days after acute onset of complete right-sided facial weakness. She has hyperacusis and decreased taste on the anterior right tongue, but no vesicles, no other cranial neuropathies, and a normal neurologic examination otherwise. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial management?
A)      Observation alone
B)      Oral corticosteroid therapy started immediately
C)       Oral antiviral therapy alone
D)      Surgical decompression of the facial nerve
E)      MRI of the internal auditory canal before any treatment
The correct response is Option B.
This is Bell palsy, and the intervention best supported by randomized evidence is a course of oral corticosteroids started as early as possible and certainly within 72 hours of onset — commonly prednisone 60 to 80 mg daily for approximately 1 week. Steroids meaningfully improve the rate of complete recovery. Antiviral therapy alone (Option C) has not been shown to be effective and is not recommended as monotherapy; adding an antiviral to steroids is commonly done and may offer a small additional benefit in patients with complete paralysis, which is why many clinicians treat with both.
The involvement of hyperacusis (stapedial branch) and dysgeusia (chorda tympani) is consistent with a lesion proximal to the stylomastoid foramen and is expected in Bell palsy; it does not change management. Observation alone (Option A) forfeits the treatment window. Surgical decompression (Option D) remains controversial and is at most considered within 2 weeks in patients with complete paralysis and greater than 90% degeneration on electroneuronography. Imaging (Option E) should not delay steroids; it is indicated for atypical features — gradual onset, no improvement by 3 to 4 months, recurrent paralysis, associated cranial neuropathies, or a mass. Eye protection with lubrication, ointment at night, and taping or a moisture chamber is essential from the first visit.
Question 5
A 39-year-old woman has narrowing of the left palpebral fissure during smiling, midfacial tightness, and involuntary neck banding 14 months after Bell palsy. Facial tone at rest is increased on the affected side. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial management?
A)      Selective neurolysis of the zygomatic branches
B)      Chemodenervation of the affected periocular and platysmal musculature combined with facial neuromuscular retraining
C)       Cross-facial nerve grafting
D)      Free gracilis transfer
E)      Complete section of the buccal branch
The correct response is Option B.
Post-paralytic synkinesis results from aberrant regeneration, in which axons destined for one muscle group reinnervate another, so that voluntary or emotional activation of one region produces unwanted co-contraction elsewhere. Increased resting tone, or hypertonicity, coexists with it. First-line treatment is nonsurgical: targeted chemodenervation with botulinum toxin to the overactive muscles — most often the orbicularis oculi, platysma, mentalis, and depressor anguli oris — combined with facial neuromuscular retraining using mirror biofeedback and, in some programs, surface electromyography. This combination is effective, reversible, and adjustable, and treatment is repeated approximately every 3 to 4 months.
Selective neurolysis (Option A) — targeted division of branches to specific antagonist muscles, most commonly the depressor complex and platysma — is a legitimate and increasingly used surgical option, but it follows a trial of chemodenervation, which also serves to predict the surgical result. Nondiscriminate section of a major branch (Option E) risks eliminating protective eye closure or creating flaccidity and is not appropriate. Cross-facial grafting and free muscle transfer (Options C and D) address flaccid paralysis with absent movement, not synkinesis with preserved but miswired innervation. Note also that in a pregnant or breastfeeding patient, retraining alone is the appropriate choice, since botulinum toxin safety is not established.
Question 6
A 6-year-old boy with Möbius syndrome has bilateral complete facial paralysis, bilateral abduction deficits, and an inability to smile. Which of the following is the most appropriate donor nerve for innervation of bilateral free muscle transfer in this patient?
A)      Contralateral facial nerve via cross-facial nerve grafting
B)      Hypoglossal nerve
C)       Nerve to masseter
D)      Spinal accessory nerve
E)      Great auricular nerve
The correct response is Option C.
Möbius syndrome is characterized by congenital bilateral facial (VII) and abducens (VI) nerve palsies, frequently with involvement of other cranial nerves and limb anomalies. The essential implication for reconstruction is that there is no normal facial nerve on either side, so cross-facial nerve grafting (Option A) is not available. The nerve to masseter is the standard donor: the trigeminal nerve is typically spared, the nerve carries a large number of motor axons, it lies immediately adjacent to the recipient site, and children in particular demonstrate a remarkable capacity for cortical adaptation, often developing a smile that becomes spontaneous over time rather than requiring conscious biting.
Bilateral free gracilis transfer, performed in staged fashion, is the reconstruction of choice, generally beginning around ages 5 to 6 when the child can participate in rehabilitation. The hypoglossal nerve (Option B) causes tongue morbidity, particularly problematic when speech and feeding are already compromised, and it is used more selectively. The spinal accessory nerve (Option D) carries shoulder morbidity. The great auricular nerve (Option E) is a sensory nerve used as a graft, not a motor donor. Comprehensive care also addresses strabismus, feeding and speech difficulties, and psychosocial support.
Question 7
A 70-year-old man with complete flaccid facial paralysis has severe right brow ptosis obscuring his superior visual field. His forehead skin is thick and deeply rhytidotic, and he has a receding hairline. He has already undergone upper eyelid weight placement. Which of the following is the most appropriate procedure for the brow?
A)      Endoscopic brow lift
B)      Coronal brow lift
C)       Direct brow lift with excision of skin immediately above the brow
D)      Botulinum toxin to the contralateral frontalis
E)      Pretrichial brow lift
The correct response is Option C.
Direct brow lift is the procedure of choice for paralytic brow ptosis in an older patient with heavy forehead tissues and an unfavorable hairline. It provides the most powerful and durable elevation because the excision is placed immediately adjacent to the structure being lifted, and the fixation is direct. The scar is placed at the superior brow margin or within a forehead rhytid, where it is generally well camouflaged in a man with deep transverse rhytids and less acceptable in a patient with smooth skin.
Endoscopic brow lift (Option A) relies on a dynamic frontalis and soft-tissue redraping and is not powerful or durable enough in a paralyzed, heavy brow. Coronal and pretrichial lifts (Options B and E) are less reliable for paralytic ptosis, produce scalp numbness, and are poor choices with a receding hairline; the pretrichial approach also raises the hairline concerns in reverse. Botulinum toxin to the normal side (Option D) can improve symmetry by weakening the contralateral frontalis, but it lowers the good brow rather than raising the affected one and does not relieve visual field obstruction. Brow correction should generally be staged with or performed after periocular procedures, since brow elevation affects upper lid position and can unmask or worsen lagophthalmos.
Question 8
A 58-year-old woman with facial paralysis has epiphora, chronic conjunctival irritation, and a lower eyelid that is displaced away from the globe. The lower lid can be distracted 12 mm from the globe and requires several blinks to return to position after downward traction. Which of the following procedures most directly addresses this finding?
A)      Lower eyelid full-thickness skin grafting
B)      Lateral tarsal strip procedure
C)       Placement of an upper eyelid platinum weight
D)      Medial canthopexy
E)      Midface lift alone
The correct response is Option B.
The distraction test (greater than 6 to 8 mm is abnormal) and the snap-back test both indicate horizontal lower eyelid laxity, which in paralytic ectropion results from loss of orbicularis tone. The lateral tarsal strip procedure directly corrects it: the lateral canthal tendon is released, a strip of tarsus is denuded of epithelium and lash follicles, shortened, and secured to the periosteum inside the lateral orbital rim, restoring horizontal tension and apposition of the lid to the globe. Correct fixation is at or slightly above the level of the pupil and inside the rim, so that the lid hugs the globe; anterior fixation leaves the lid tented away from the eye.
An upper eyelid weight (Option C) treats lagophthalmos and is usually combined with lower lid tightening, but it does not address ectropion. Skin grafting (Option A) treats cicatricial ectropion from anterior lamellar shortage, which is not the mechanism here. Medial canthopexy (Option D) addresses punctal eversion and medial canthal laxity and may be a useful adjunct when the punctum is malpositioned, but the dominant problem described is generalized horizontal laxity. A midface lift (Option E) is a valuable adjunct in paralysis with midface descent and negative vector but does not by itself correct lid laxity. Corneal protection remains the overriding priority throughout.
Question 9
A 29-year-old man sustains a laceration of the left cheek from a knife. Exploration in the operating room shows a transected buccal branch of the facial nerve 1 cm medial to a vertical line dropped from the lateral canthus. Which of the following is the most appropriate management of this nerve injury?
A)      Primary epineurial repair under the operating microscope
B)      Interposition nerve grafting with the great auricular nerve
C)       Repair with a nerve conduit
D)      No repair of the nerve
E)      Immediate masseteric nerve transfer
The correct response is Option D.
The conventional teaching is that facial nerve branches injured medial to a vertical line dropped from the lateral canthus need not be repaired, because at that point the branches are small, highly arborized, and richly cross-innervated, and clinically significant deficits generally do not result. Injuries at or lateral to that line should be explored and repaired primarily whenever possible.
Two practical caveats belong with this rule. First, the marginal mandibular branch and the frontal (temporal) branch have little or no cross-innervation, so injuries to those two branches should be repaired regardless of the medial-lateral rule when they are identifiable. Second, the window for using a nerve stimulator to identify distal branches closes at roughly 72 hours after injury, before Wallerian degeneration renders the distal segment unexcitable — which argues for early exploration of any suspected proximal facial nerve laceration.
When repair is indicated, tension-free primary epineurial coaptation under magnification (Option A) is ideal, with interposition grafting from the great auricular or sural nerve (Option B) for gaps, and conduits (Option C) reasonable for short gaps under roughly 3 cm. Nerve transfer (Option E) is not needed when a proximal stump is available. Concomitant parotid duct injury should be sought, since the buccal branch runs with the duct.
Question 10
A 45-year-old woman with complete facial paralysis has 5 mm of lagophthalmos and exposure keratopathy. Placement of an upper eyelid weight is planned. Trial weights are taped to the eyelid preoperatively. Which of the following criteria should determine the weight selected?
A)      The heaviest weight that produces complete eyelid closure at rest
B)      The lightest weight that brings the upper lid to within 2 to 4 mm of the lower lid with full corneal coverage on relaxed closure
C)       The weight that produces the fastest blink
D)      The weight that achieves symmetry with the contralateral upper eyelid position
E)      A standard 1.2-g weight regardless of trial results
The correct response is Option B.
Weight selection is a trade-off between corneal protection and visual field. The correct endpoint is the lightest weight that covers the cornea and brings the upper lid to within a few millimeters of the lower lid with the patient relaxed. Chasing complete closure (Option A) is the classic error: the resulting over-weighted lid produces ptosis, obstructs the superior visual field, and is a frequent reason for reoperation. Neither symmetry with the normal eyelid (Option D) nor blink speed (Option C) is achievable — a passive gravity-dependent implant will always close more slowly and sit differently than a normally innervated lid — and neither should drive selection. A fixed default weight (Option E) ignores individual variation in lid mass, skin thickness, and levator tone.
Technique points that accompany this decision: the implant is placed in the pretarsal plane, deep to the orbicularis and superficial to the levator aponeurosis and tarsus, with the inferior edge 2 to 3 mm above the lash line and centered over the medial limbus; it is fixed to tarsus with nonabsorbable suture and covered by orbicularis to limit visibility and extrusion. Platinum is increasingly preferred over gold for its greater density, thinner profile, and lower rates of allergy and capsule formation. Lower lid laxity should be corrected concurrently, since lagophthalmos is a function of both lids.
Eye Aesthetic and Reconstructive
Question 1
A 74-year-old man undergoes Mohs excision of a basal cell carcinoma of the left upper eyelid, resulting in a full-thickness defect involving 80% of the horizontal eyelid width. The lower eyelid is uninvolved. Which of the following is the most appropriate reconstruction?
A)      Full-thickness skin grafting
B)      Cutler-Beard bridge flap from the lower eyelid, divided at 4 to 6 weeks
C)       Tenzel semicircular rotation flap
D)      Hughes tarsoconjunctival flap from the lower eyelid
E)      Primary closure with lateral canthotomy and cantholysis
The correct response is Option B.
The Cutler-Beard flap is the standard reconstruction for full-thickness upper eyelid defects exceeding roughly two thirds of the lid width. A full-thickness advancement flap of skin, muscle, and conjunctiva is raised from the lower eyelid beneath an intact bridge of lower lid margin (which preserves the lower lid’s marginal architecture and blood supply) and passed under that bridge into the upper lid defect. Because the flap carries no tarsus, a cartilage or tarsal graft is usually interposed to provide posterior lamellar rigidity. The pedicle is divided at 4 to 6 weeks in a second stage. The principal drawback is that the eye is occluded during the interval, which is why the technique is avoided in children at risk for deprivation amblyopia and in monocular patients when alternatives exist.
Primary closure with canthotomy and cantholysis (Option E) handles defects up to roughly 30 to 40%, more in elderly patients with lax lids. A Tenzel semicircular flap (Option C) extends that to about 60% but recruits only anterior lamella and requires a separate posterior lamellar graft for a full-thickness defect. The Hughes flap (Option D) is the mirror-image procedure — a tarsoconjunctival flap advanced from the upper lid to reconstruct the lower lid. Skin grafting alone (Option A) cannot reconstruct a full-thickness defect.
Question 2
A 68-year-old woman has a full-thickness lower eyelid defect involving 60% of the horizontal width following tumor excision. The lateral canthus is intact and the remaining lid tissues are lax. Which of the following is the most appropriate reconstruction?
A)      Primary closure without canthal release
B)      Tenzel semicircular advancement flap with lateral canthotomy and inferior cantholysis
C)       Cutler-Beard flap
D)      Free tarsoconjunctival graft with a full-thickness skin graft over it
E)      Mustardé cheek rotation flap with a posterior lamellar graft
The correct response is Option B.
The reconstruction of eyelid defects follows a size-based algorithm, and both lamellae must be accounted for. For a lower lid defect of roughly 40 to 60%, a Tenzel semicircular flap — a laterally based, superiorly arcing musculocutaneous rotation flap combined with lateral canthotomy and inferior cantholysis — mobilizes enough tissue for tension-free single-stage closure while recruiting some conjunctiva and preserving the lid margin contour. Defects under about 30 to 40% (more in a lax, elderly lid) close primarily, sometimes with a canthotomy alone (Option A is inadequate at 60%). Defects exceeding roughly two thirds require either a Hughes tarsoconjunctival flap from the upper lid with an anterior lamellar skin graft, a free tarsoconjunctival or auricular cartilage graft with a vascularized anterior lamellar flap (Option D fails the rule that both lamellae cannot be grafts, since one layer must bring its own blood supply), or a Mustardé cheek rotation flap with a posterior lamellar graft (Option E) for very large defects extending onto the cheek.
The Cutler-Beard flap (Option C) reconstructs the upper lid, not the lower. Whatever the technique, the reconstruction must restore lid margin apposition to the globe, adequate horizontal tension, and a smooth mucosal surface, and must avoid vertical tension that produces ectropion or retraction.
Question 3
A 61-year-old woman calls the office 6 hours after upper and lower blepharoplasty reporting severe, deep right orbital pain, decreasing vision, and progressive swelling. Examination shows marked proptosis, a tense orbit, chemosis, an ecchymotic tight lid, and an afferent pupillary defect. Which of the following is the most appropriate immediate action?
A)      Application of ice and administration of intravenous mannitol, with reassessment in 1 hour
B)      Urgent CT of the orbits to confirm the diagnosis
C)       Immediate lateral canthotomy and inferior cantholysis at the bedside
D)      Urgent ophthalmology consultation with observation pending arrival
E)      Administration of intravenous corticosteroids and acetazolamide
The correct response is Option C.
Retrobulbar hematoma after blepharoplasty is an orbital compartment syndrome, and vision loss is time-dependent — the tolerance of the retina and optic nerve to ischemia is measured in the range of 60 to 100 minutes. The triad of pain, proptosis, and vision loss with a tense orbit and afferent pupillary defect is the diagnosis; it is made clinically, not radiographically. The immediate maneuver is bedside lateral canthotomy with inferior cantholysis, which releases the lower lid from the orbital rim, allows the globe to move forward, and decompresses the orbit. Superior cantholysis is added if pressure remains elevated. This is followed by opening the surgical incisions, evacuating clot, and controlling bleeding, usually in the operating room, with adjunctive medical measures — head elevation, mannitol, acetazolamide, corticosteroids, and topical beta-blockade — as supportive but never primary therapy.
Any option that inserts a delay (Options A, B, D, and E) risks permanent blindness. Prevention matters as much as management: meticulous hemostasis, avoidance of blind deep cautery, control of blood pressure and pain on emergence, cessation of antiplatelet and anticoagulant agents where medically permissible, and clear postoperative instructions to the patient to report pain and visual change immediately rather than waiting.
Question 4
A 5-year-old boy has severe congenital ptosis of the right upper eyelid with a margin-to-reflex distance of −1 mm and levator function measured at 2 mm. The eyelid obscures the visual axis. Which of the following procedures is most appropriate?
A)      Müller muscle-conjunctival resection
B)      Fasanella-Servat procedure
C)       Levator advancement
D)      Frontalis suspension with autologous fascia lata or a synthetic sling
E)      Observation until age 12 years
The correct response is Option D.
Ptosis surgery is selected primarily on the basis of levator function, measured as upper lid excursion from downgaze to upgaze with the frontalis immobilized. Function of 10 mm or more is good, 5 to 10 mm is fair, and less than 4 to 5 mm is poor. With poor function, no amount of levator shortening will produce adequate excursion, and the eyelid must be suspended from the frontalis muscle so that brow elevation raises the lid. Autologous fascia lata is the most durable sling material and is preferred in older children in whom it can be harvested; synthetic materials such as silicone rods or polypropylene are used in young children and have the advantage of adjustability, at the cost of higher rates of extrusion and recurrence.
Levator advancement or resection (Option C) is appropriate for fair to good function. Müller muscle-conjunctival resection and the Fasanella-Servat procedure (Options A and B) are posterior approaches for mild ptosis with good levator function, typically selected in patients whose lid elevates in response to phenylephrine testing. Observation (Option E) is unacceptable when the visual axis is occluded, because deprivation amblyopia develops during the critical period and may be irreversible; ptosis obstructing the pupil or inducing significant astigmatism warrants early surgery. Patients and families must be counseled that lagophthalmos and the need for lubrication are expected after frontalis suspension.
Question 5
A 47-year-old woman requests lower blepharoplasty for “bags.” Examination shows prominent globes, a negative vector with the malar eminence posterior to the anterior corneal plane, scleral show of 1 mm bilaterally, and a lower lid distraction of 9 mm with a delayed snap-back. Which of the following adjunctive maneuvers is most important to include in her operative plan?
A)      Aggressive skin-muscle flap excision
B)      Transcutaneous fat removal alone
C)       Lateral canthal support with canthopexy or canthoplasty
D)      Full-thickness skin grafting of the lower lid
E)      Botulinum toxin to the pretarsal orbicularis
The correct response is Option C.
Three features in this examination — a negative vector, preexisting scleral show, and demonstrable horizontal lid laxity — together identify a patient at high risk for postoperative lower lid malposition, and each independently argues for canthal support. Without it, even a modest lower blepharoplasty can produce scleral show, lid retraction, rounding of the canthus, or frank ectropion, because the healing lower lid is pulled inferiorly by scar contracture against inadequate horizontal tension and inadequate support from the prominent globe below.
Canthopexy tightens and repositions the existing canthal tendon without disinserting it and is appropriate for mild to moderate laxity; canthoplasty, typically a lateral tarsal strip, disinserts, shortens, and reattaches the lid and is required for more severe laxity. Aggressive skin-muscle excision (Option A) is precisely what should be avoided in this patient; conservative skin removal, or a transconjunctival approach with skin pinch and laser or chemical resurfacing, is safer. Fat removal alone (Option B) misses the malposition risk, and in a negative-vector patient fat repositioning over the rim is usually preferable to excision, which can hollow the lid. Question 6
A 54-year-old woman is bothered by a groove extending from the medial canthus obliquely across the inferior orbital rim, with an apparent bulge above it. She has minimal skin excess and no lower eyelid laxity. Release of which of the following structures is most directly responsible for improving this contour?
A)      Capsulopalpebral fascia
B)      Lateral canthal tendon
C)       Orbital septum at the arcus marginalis and the orbicularis retaining (orbitomalar) ligament
D)      Whitnall ligament
E)      Levator aponeurosis
The correct response is Option C.
The tear trough and the palpebromalar groove mark the fixed attachment of the orbicularis retaining ligament, a bilaminar membrane running from the periosteum of the inferior orbital rim to the undersurface of the orbicularis, with the tear trough ligament forming its medial component. With age, orbital fat pseudoherniates above this tethered line while the tissue below descends, producing the double-convexity contour of a bulge above a groove. Releasing the arcus marginalis and the orbicularis retaining ligament frees the tethered skin and allows the herniated fat to be transposed as a pedicled graft over the rim into the resulting subperiosteal or supraperiosteal pocket, converting a step-off into a smooth lid-cheek junction. The maneuver can be performed transconjunctivally or transcutaneously.
The capsulopalpebral fascia (Option A) is the lower lid retractor, incised to gain access in the transconjunctival approach but not the structure responsible for the groove. The lateral canthal tendon (Option B) is addressed for lid support and repositioning, not for fat transposition. Whitnall ligament and the levator aponeurosis (Options D and E) are upper eyelid structures. Fat excision without addressing the ligament typically deepens the trough by hollowing the area above it.
Question 7

Question 8

A)      Layered repair of the eyelid alone, with dacryocystorhinostomy if epiphora develops
B)      Repair of the canaliculus over a silicone stent with layered eyelid and lid margin repair

D)      Punctoplasty alone
E)      Repair delayed 7 to 10 days to allow the bite wound to declare
The correct response is Option B.
Any laceration medial to the punctum, and many that involve the medial third of the lid, should raise suspicion for canalicular injury, and the canaliculus should be probed and irrigated to confirm. Question 9

B)      Inferior oblique muscle
C)       Superior oblique tendon

Perioperative Care
Question 1
A 209-lb (95-kg) woman with no drug allergies is scheduled to undergo a deep inferior epigastric perforator flap breast reconstruction expected to last 8 hours. Estimated blood loss is anticipated to be 300 mL. Which of the following regimens for cefazolin prophylaxis is most appropriate?
A)      1 g given 5 minutes before incision, with no redosing
B)      1 g given 45 minutes before incision, redosed after 300 mL of blood loss
C)       2 g given 45 minutes before incision, redosed at approximately 4 hours
D)      2 g given 90 minutes before incision, with no redosing
E)      2 g given at the time of incision, continued every 8 hours for 24 hours postoperatively
The correct response is Option C.
Three variables define appropriate cefazolin prophylaxis, and this question tests all three.
Dose is weight-based: 1 g for patients under 80 kg, 2 g for patients over 80 kg, and 3 g is commonly recommended above 120 kg. A 95-kg patient receives 2 g.
Timing must allow adequate tissue concentration at incision. The accepted window is within 60 minutes of incision, and the narrowest evidence-supported interval is 30 to 59 minutes before incision. Administration at or after incision (Option E) and within a few minutes of it (Option A) does not achieve therapeutic tissue levels, while giving it more than 2 hours ahead allows levels to fall.
Redosing is indicated for procedures exceeding roughly two half-lives of the agent — approximately 4 hours for cefazolin — or for blood loss greater than about 1500 mL. An 8-hour operation therefore warrants an intraoperative redose; 300 mL of blood loss does not (Option B).
Prophylaxis should be discontinued at wound closure or within 24 hours; prolonged postoperative courses do not reduce surgical site infection and increase resistance and Clostridioides difficile risk. Question 2
A 34-year-old woman with systemic lupus erythematosus who has taken prednisone daily for 6 years is scheduled to undergo excision of a soft-tissue lesion of the back with local flap closure. She is well nourished, with a normal albumin concentration and a body mass index of 24 kg/m2. Perioperative supplementation with which of the following is most likely to improve her wound healing?
A)      Vitamin A
B)      Vitamin B12
C)       Vitamin C
D)      Vitamin D
E)      Zinc
The correct response is Option A.
Corticosteroids impair every phase of wound healing — they suppress the inflammatory response and macrophage recruitment, inhibit fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis, and reduce wound tensile strength. One proposed mechanism is stabilization of lysosomal membranes. Vitamin A has the opposite, destabilizing effect on lysosomal membranes and restores the inflammatory response and collagen deposition, reversing most of the steroid effect on healing — with the notable exceptions of wound contraction and resistance to infection, which are not corrected. Typical regimens are 10,000 to 25,000 IU orally daily for 5 to 14 days perioperatively; topical regimens have also been described. Supplementation should be time-limited, since chronic high-dose vitamin A is hepatotoxic and teratogenic, and it should be discussed with the physician managing the underlying disease, since it may antagonize the desired anti-inflammatory effect.
The other micronutrients matter only when deficient. Vitamin C deficiency impairs collagen cross-linking and causes scurvy, and zinc deficiency impairs epithelialization and fibroblast proliferation — but supplementing a well-nourished patient who is not deficient provides no benefit. Vitamin B12 and vitamin D (Options B and D) have no established role in perioperative wound healing in a replete patient. Stress-dose steroid coverage should be considered separately for a patient on long-term suppressive therapy.
Question 3
A 58-year-old man with no known cardiac disease is scheduled to undergo elective ventral hernia repair with component separation. He walks 18 holes of golf weekly and climbs two flights of stairs without dyspnea or chest pain. He takes no medications, has never smoked, and has a normal physical examination and a normal 12-lead electrocardiogram. Which of the following is the most appropriate preoperative cardiac evaluation?
A)      Proceed to surgery without further cardiac testing
B)      Exercise treadmill stress testing
C)       Dobutamine stress echocardiography
D)      Coronary computed tomographic angiography
E)      Cardiac catheterization
The correct response is Option A.
Preoperative cardiac evaluation follows a stepwise algorithm. First, exclude an active or unstable cardiac condition — unstable coronary syndrome, decompensated heart failure, significant arrhythmia, or severe valvular disease — which would require evaluation and treatment regardless of the planned operation. Second, assess the surgical risk. Third, estimate functional capacity in metabolic equivalents. A patient who can achieve 4 METs or more — climbing a flight of stairs, walking up a hill, or performing moderate recreational activity such as golf — without symptoms may proceed to elective intermediate-risk surgery without further cardiac testing, regardless of the presence of most clinical risk factors. This patient has no risk factors and clearly exceeds 4 METs.
Additional noninvasive testing (Options B, C, and D) is reserved for patients with elevated risk and poor or unknown functional capacity, and only when the result would change management — that is, when it might lead to revascularization or a substantive alteration in perioperative care. Reflexive stress testing delays care, generates false positives, and has not been shown to improve outcomes in low-risk patients. Cardiac catheterization (Option E) is an invasive study reserved for patients with positive noninvasive testing or acute coronary syndrome; it should not be pursued as a screening study before elective surgery.
Question 4
A 48-year-old woman with a body mass index of 33 kg/m2 is scheduled to undergo abdominoplasty combined with bilateral augmentation mastopexy, with an expected operative time of 5 hours under general anesthesia. She takes an oral contraceptive and has a history of a superficial thrombophlebitis. Which of the following is the most appropriate approach to venous thromboembolism prophylaxis?
A)      Early ambulation alone
B)      Sequential compression devices intraoperatively only
C)       Risk stratification with a validated score, sequential compression devices, and postoperative chemoprophylaxis
D)      Preoperative placement of an inferior vena cava filter
E)      Therapeutic-dose anticoagulation for 30 days postoperatively
The correct response is Option C.
Venous thromboembolism is a leading cause of preventable death after aesthetic surgery, and body contouring combined with other procedures carries the highest risk in plastic surgery. Care should begin with a validated risk assessment — the Caprini score is the tool most widely applied and validated in plastic surgery populations. This patient accumulates points for age, obesity, oral contraceptive use, an operation longer than 45 minutes, general anesthesia, and a history of superficial venous thrombosis, placing her in a high-risk category.
Recommended practice combines mechanical prophylaxis with sequential compression devices applied before inductionand continued until ambulation, early and frequent ambulation, avoidance of excessive intraoperative hip flexion and postoperative flexed positioning, maintenance of normothermia and euvolemia, and — for high-risk patients — postoperative chemoprophylaxis with low-molecular-weight or unfractionated heparin, weighed against hematoma risk. Discontinuing estrogen-containing contraceptives preoperatively is commonly advised.
Mechanical measures alone (Options A and B) are insufficient for a high-risk patient. A vena cava filter (Option D) is not a prophylactic measure for elective surgery. Therapeutic anticoagulation (Option E) is treatment, not prophylaxis, and would create unacceptable bleeding risk. Combining multiple procedures substantially raises complication rates and warrants explicit informed consent.
Question 5
A 45-year-old man who smokes one pack of cigarettes daily requests abdominoplasty. He is willing to stop smoking. Which of the following recommendations regarding preoperative smoking cessation is most appropriate?
A)      Cessation for 24 hours is adequate because carboxyhemoglobin normalizes within that period
B)      Cessation for at least 4 weeks before surgery is recommended to reduce wound-healing complications
C)       Substitution of nicotine replacement therapy is equivalent to complete cessation for wound healing
D)      Cessation confers no benefit once a patient has smoked for more than 10 years
E)      Cessation should begin only after surgery, since preoperative cessation increases pulmonary secretions and risk
The correct response is Option B.
Smoking impairs healing through several mechanisms: nicotine causes cutaneous vasoconstriction and increases platelet aggregation; carbon monoxide binds hemoglobin with far greater affinity than oxygen, reducing oxygen delivery; and hydrogen cyanide impairs oxidative metabolism. The result is an increase in flap necrosis, wound dehiscence, infection, and delayed healing — risks that are substantial in operations that rely on random-pattern perfusion of undermined skin flaps, such as abdominoplasty, face lift, and reduction mammaplasty.
Some effects reverse quickly: carboxyhemoglobin levels normalize within roughly 12 hours to a day, and nicotine clears in days (Option A confuses this partial recovery with adequate preparation). But the immunologic and proliferative deficits require longer, and the commonly recommended interval is a minimum of 4 weeks of complete cessation before and after surgery, with some surgeons requiring 6 weeks and confirming abstinence with urine or serum cotinine testing.
Nicotine replacement (Option C) maintains the vasoconstrictive and platelet effects and is not equivalent to abstinence, although it is preferable to continued smoking. The claim in Option D is false — cessation benefits patients regardless of smoking duration. Option E inverts the evidence; while a transient increase in secretions occurs, preoperative cessation reduces both pulmonary and wound complications.
Question 6
A 66-year-old man with hypertension and coronary artery disease takes metoprolol succinate daily. He is scheduled to undergo elective reconstruction of a scalp defect under general anesthesia. Which of the following is the most appropriate management of his beta-blocker therapy?
A)      Discontinue it 48 hours before surgery to avoid intraoperative bradycardia
B)      Continue it through the perioperative period without interruption
C)       Discontinue it and start a calcium channel blocker on the morning of surgery
D)      Hold the dose on the morning of surgery and resume on postoperative day 3
E)      Double the dose on the morning of surgery to blunt the surgical stress response
The correct response is Option B.
Beta-blockers should be continued perioperatively in patients already taking them chronically. Abrupt withdrawal produces rebound tachycardia, hypertension, and a documented increase in myocardial ischemia, infarction, and mortality — the harm of stopping is well established, whereas the concern about intraoperative bradycardia is manageable with standard anesthetic monitoring.
The corollary, equally important, is that beta-blockers should not be started de novo on the day of surgery in beta-blocker-naive patients. Large trial data demonstrated that high-dose beta-blockade initiated immediately preoperatively reduced myocardial infarction but increased stroke and total mortality, largely through hypotension and bradycardia. When beta-blockade is genuinely indicated for a patient with elevated risk, it should be initiated well in advance — ideally more than a week, and preferably 30 days — and titrated to heart rate and blood pressure.
Options A and D risk withdrawal phenomena. Substituting a different class (Option C) does not prevent withdrawal and introduces new variables. Dose escalation on the day of surgery (Option E) reproduces exactly the strategy that increased stroke and mortality. Other chronic medications generally continued perioperatively include statins, which reduce perioperative cardiac events; angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers are more often held on the morning of surgery because of refractory intraoperative hypotension.
Question 7
A 39-year-old woman undergoing liposuction of the trunk and thighs receives tumescent infiltration. Approximately 45 minutes after infiltration is completed, she reports perioral tingling and tinnitus, then becomes agitated and has a generalized seizure followed by a wide-complex bradycardic rhythm. Which of the following should be administered after airway control and initiation of advanced cardiac life support?
A)      20% lipid emulsion, 1.5 mL/kg bolus followed by an infusion
B)      Sodium bicarbonate, 1 mEq/kg
C)       Flumazenil
D)      High-dose epinephrine, 1 mg boluses
E)      Dantrolene, 2.5 mg/kg
The correct response is Option A.
This is local anesthetic systemic toxicity, which classically progresses from prodromal central nervous system excitation — perioral numbness, tinnitus, metallic taste, agitation — to seizures and then cardiovascular collapse. Lidocaine toxicity after tumescent infiltration is characteristically delayed, with peak plasma levels occurring 8 to 12 hours after infiltration because of the vasoconstriction and slow absorption produced by dilute epinephrine, so symptoms may appear well after the patient leaves the operating room. Maximum recommended tumescent lidocaine doses of roughly 35 to 55 mg/kg reflect this pharmacokinetic profile, in contrast with much lower limits for standard infiltration.
Treatment is airway management with 100% oxygen, benzodiazepines for seizures, and prompt administration of 20% lipid emulsion — 1.5 mL/kg bolus, repeated as needed, followed by an infusion of 0.25 mL/kg/min — which sequesters lipophilic anesthetic and restores cardiac function. Modifications to standard resuscitation include using reducedepinephrine doses (≤1 mcg/kg) rather than standard 1-mg boluses (Option D), and avoiding vasopressin, calcium channel blockers, beta-blockers, and additional local anesthetics such as lidocaine for arrhythmia. Cardiopulmonary bypass should be considered for refractory collapse.
Bicarbonate (Option B) is used for tricyclic-type sodium channel toxicity but is not the primary therapy here. Flumazenil (Option C) reverses benzodiazepines. Dantrolene (Option E) treats malignant hyperthermia.
Question 8
Thirty minutes into a general anesthetic for bilateral reduction mammaplasty, a 24-year-old woman develops masseter rigidity, tachycardia, an end-tidal carbon dioxide concentration that rises from 38 to 74 mmHg despite increased minute ventilation, and a temperature that climbs from 36.8°C to 39.4°C. Which of the following is the most important immediate treatment?
A)      Active surface cooling and cooled intravenous fluids
B)      Intravenous dantrolene
C)       Intravenous labetalol
D)      Intravenous sodium bicarbonate
E)      Discontinuation of nitrous oxide with continuation of the volatile agent at a lower concentration
The correct response is Option B.
Malignant hyperthermia is a hypermetabolic crisis triggered by volatile anesthetics and succinylcholine in genetically susceptible individuals, most commonly through mutations of the ryanodine receptor that cause uncontrolled calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Rising end-tidal carbon dioxide despite adequate ventilation is typically the earliest and most sensitive sign; masseter rigidity, tachycardia, hyperthermia, rigidity, acidosis, hyperkalemia, and myoglobinuria follow.
Dantrolene is the only definitive therapy: it blocks calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum and interrupts the hypermetabolic cascade. It is given at 2.5 mg/kg intravenously, repeated to a typical maximum of 10 mg/kg and beyond if needed, and continued after the acute episode because recrudescence occurs in a substantial minority of patients. The reconstituted-in-water formulation requires many vials and considerable mixing time, which is why concentrated formulations and a rehearsed team response matter.
Simultaneous measures — not substitutes — include immediately discontinuing all volatile agents and succinylcholine (Option E is therefore wrong), hyperventilating with 100% oxygen at high flows, active cooling (Option A), treating hyperkalemia and acidosis (Option D), managing arrhythmias while avoiding calcium channel blockers, monitoring for rhabdomyolysis, and calling the Malignant Hyperthermia Association hotline. Labetalol (Option C) treats the tachycardia symptomatically without addressing the mechanism.
Question 9
A 62-year-old man with type 2 diabetes mellitus is scheduled to undergo elective free tissue transfer for a lower extremity wound. His hemoglobin A1c is 10.2%, and home glucose readings range from 220 to 340 mg/dL. He is otherwise stable and the wound is not infected. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A)      Proceed with surgery as scheduled and manage glucose with a postoperative insulin infusion
B)      Defer elective surgery and refer for optimization of glycemic control, targeting improved hemoglobin A1c
C)       Proceed with surgery and begin an oral hypoglycemic agent on the morning of surgery
D)      Proceed with surgery and target a perioperative glucose concentration below 100 mg/dL with an intensive insulin protocol
E)      Cancel surgery permanently because free tissue transfer is contraindicated in diabetes
The correct response is Option B.
Poor glycemic control is a modifiable risk factor for surgical site infection, wound-healing failure, and overall complications. Elevated hemoglobin A1c — thresholds around 7.5 to 8% are commonly cited, with substantially increased risk above 8% — and perioperative hyperglycemia above roughly 180 to 200 mg/dL are both independently associated with worse outcomes. For an elective operation with a substantial reconstructive investment, deferring to optimize control is appropriate and is one of the few interventions that reliably improves the odds.
Proceeding without optimization (Options A and C) accepts avoidable risk, and starting an oral agent on the day of surgery accomplishes nothing. Option D describes the opposite error: intensive insulin protocols targeting near-normal glucose have been associated with dangerous hypoglycemia and increased mortality in critically ill patients, and the accepted perioperative target is roughly 140 to 180 mg/dL, not below 100. Option E overstates the case — diabetes is not a contraindication to free tissue transfer, and well-controlled diabetic patients have good outcomes.
Other elements of optimization in this patient include nutritional assessment, vascular evaluation of the recipient limb, smoking cessation, and off-loading of the wound.
Question 10
A 71-year-old woman is scheduled to undergo Mohs reconstruction of a nasal defect under local anesthesia with sedation. She takes warfarin for atrial fibrillation with a mechanical mitral valve. Which of the following is the most appropriate perioperative management of her anticoagulation?
A)      Discontinue warfarin 5 days before surgery without bridging
B)      Continue warfarin at her usual dose with confirmation of a therapeutic international normalized ratio
C)       Discontinue warfarin and administer vitamin K on the morning of surgery
D)      Discontinue warfarin and transfuse fresh frozen plasma preoperatively
E)      Switch to a direct oral anticoagulant 3 days before surgery
The correct response is Option B.
For cutaneous and small soft-tissue procedures — Mohs reconstruction, skin excisions, and most minor dermatologic surgery — continuing anticoagulation is generally recommended. The bleeding encountered is controllable with local measures such as pressure, electrocautery, and careful layered closure, whereas interrupting anticoagulation in a patient with a mechanical mitral valve carries a meaningful risk of thromboembolism and stroke. For larger operations with substantial undermining or dead space, a formal interruption plan should be made with cardiology, weighing hematoma risk against thrombotic risk.

Question 1
A 52-year-old man with a severe traumatic brain injury and multiple facial fractures has been receiving mechanical ventilation and enteral nutrition for 12 days. Indirect calorimetry is performed and shows a respiratory quotient of 1.2. Which of the following is the most appropriate interpretation and response?
A)      The patient is underfed; increase caloric delivery
B)      The patient is in ketosis; increase carbohydrate delivery
C)       The patient is being overfed with net lipogenesis; reduce caloric delivery
D)      The patient is oxidizing predominantly lipid; increase carbohydrate delivery
E)      The value reflects predominantly protein oxidation; increase protein delivery
The correct response is Option C.
The respiratory quotient is the ratio of carbon dioxide produced to oxygen consumed (VCO2/VO2), measured by indirect calorimetry, and it identifies which substrate is being oxidized. The reference values are worth memorizing: pure lipid oxidation yields approximately 0.7, protein approximately 0.8, and pure carbohydrate oxidation 1.0. Values above 1.0cannot be explained by substrate oxidation alone and indicate net lipogenesis — the conversion of excess carbohydrate to fat, a process that generates carbon dioxide — and therefore signify overfeeding.
The optimal range for a critically ill patient receiving nutritional support is roughly 0.85 to 0.95, reflecting a balanced mix of lipid and carbohydrate oxidation. Values below about 0.8 suggest underfeeding with mobilization of endogenous fat stores. A value of 1.2 mandates reduction of caloric delivery.
The clinical consequence of overfeeding is directly relevant in a ventilated patient: excess carbon dioxide production increases minute ventilation requirements and can impede weaning, and overfeeding also contributes to hepatic steatosis and hyperglycemia. Indirect calorimetry also yields resting energy expenditure via the Weir equation, which is more accurate than predictive formulas in critically ill and burn patients, in whom hypermetabolism is profound and difficult to estimate. Ketosis is not measured by the respiratory quotient (Option B).
Question 2
A 67-year-old man in the intensive care unit after a prolonged reconstructive procedure has acute kidney injury. Serum potassium concentration is 7.1 mEq/L. Electrocardiography shows peaked T waves, widening of the QRS complex, and loss of P waves. Which of the following should be administered first?
A)      Intravenous calcium gluconate
B)      Intravenous regular insulin with dextrose
C)       Nebulized albuterol
D)      Oral sodium polystyrene sulfonate
E)      Intravenous furosemide
The correct response is Option A.
When hyperkalemia is accompanied by electrocardiographic changes, the immediate threat is not the serum potassium concentration but cardiac conduction failure progressing through a sinusoidal QRS-T pattern to ventricular fibrillation and asystole. Intravenous calcium — gluconate or chloride — is given first because it antagonizes the effect of potassium at the myocyte membrane, restoring the transmembrane potential gradient and stabilizing conduction within 1 to 3 minutes. Crucially, calcium does not lower the serum potassium at all; it buys time. Its effect lasts only 30 to 60 minutes and may need to be repeated.
The therapies that actually move potassium follow immediately: insulin with dextrose (Option B) and high-dose nebulized beta-2 agonists (Option C) shift potassium intracellularly within 10 to 30 minutes, and sodium bicarbonate has a role in acidemia. Then come the therapies that remove potassium from the body: loop diuretics (Option E), cation exchange or potassium-binding resins (Option D), and dialysis, which is definitive in oliguric renal failure.
The organizing framework — stabilize, shift, eliminate — makes the sequencing question easy. Note also that calcium chloride delivers roughly three times the elemental calcium of gluconate but is sclerosing and should be given through a central line, and that calcium should be used cautiously in digoxin toxicity.
Question 3
A 9-year-old boy is in the postanesthesia care unit following microtia reconstruction with costal cartilage harvest. During the harvest, the pleura was entered and repaired, and an intraoperative chest radiograph showed no pneumothorax. He now becomes acutely dyspneic and agitated, with a heart rate of 155 beats/min, a blood pressure of 74/40 mmHg, and an oxygen saturation of 84% on a nonrebreather mask. Breath sounds are absent on the right with hyperresonance to percussion, and the trachea is deviated to the left. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?
A)      Obtain a portable chest radiograph
B)      Obtain an arterial blood gas
C)       Perform needle decompression of the right chest
D)      Perform endotracheal intubation
E)      Reopen the chest wall incision in the postanesthesia care unit
The correct response is Option C.
Tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis and must be treated before imaging. Air accumulates in the pleural space through a one-way mechanism, intrapleural pressure rises with each breath, the mediastinum shifts, and venous return falls — the hypotension is obstructive shock, not primarily a ventilation problem. The classic findings are all present: respiratory distress, absent breath sounds and hyperresonance on the affected side, tracheal deviation away from the lesion, tachycardia, and hypotension; jugular venous distention may be present unless the patient is hypovolemic.
Immediate needle decompression with a large-bore catheter, followed by tube thoracostomy, is the treatment. Traditional teaching places the needle in the second intercostal space at the midclavicular line, though the fourth or fifth intercostal space at the anterior axillary line is increasingly favored in adults because chest wall thickness at the anterior site often exceeds standard catheter length.
Radiography and blood gas analysis (Options A and B) consume time the patient does not have. Intubation (Option D) does not relieve the tension and, by adding positive pressure ventilation, can worsen it dramatically. Reopening the incision (Option E) is slower and requires resources not immediately available. A negative intraoperative radiograph does not exclude a pneumothorax that develops later, particularly once positive pressure is withdrawn.
Question 4
A 41-year-old woman with type 1 von Willebrand disease is scheduled to undergo reduction mammaplasty. Preoperative administration of desmopressin is planned. Measurement of which of the following, in addition to von Willebrand factor ristocetin cofactor activity, is most important in determining the adequacy of her response and the appropriate dose?
A)      Factor II activity
B)      Factor V activity
C)       Factor VII activity
D)      Factor VIII activity
E)      Factor X activity
The correct response is Option D.
Von Willebrand factor has two distinct functions: it mediates platelet adhesion to subendothelium at sites of injury, and it binds and stabilizes factor VIII in the circulation. Consequently, deficiency of von Willebrand factor produces a secondary reduction in factor VIII, and both must be measured to assess hemostatic adequacy. The standard laboratory panel comprises von Willebrand factor antigen, ristocetin cofactor activity (a measure of function), and factor VIII activity.
Desmopressin is a synthetic vasopressin analog that triggers release of stored von Willebrand factor from Weibel-Palade bodies of endothelial cells, raising both von Willebrand factor and factor VIII levels. It is most effective in type 1 disease, which accounts for roughly three quarters of cases; it is variably effective or contraindicated in type 2 subtypes (particularly type 2B, where it can worsen thrombocytopenia) and ineffective in type 3, which requires von Willebrand factor concentrate. A preoperative trial of desmopressin with measurement of the response identifies responders.
Two practical points: levels are generally targeted above 50 IU/dL for surgery, and excessive elevation — ristocetin cofactor above about 200 IU/dL or factor VIII above about 250 IU/dL — is associated with thrombosis. Because desmopressin is antidiuretic, fluid restriction is required to prevent hyponatremia and seizures. The other listed factors (Options A, B, C, and E) are unrelated to isolated von Willebrand disease.
Question 5
A 36-year-old woman is scheduled to undergo bilateral reduction mammaplasty, and the surgeon plans to administer tranexamic acid intravenously to reduce blood loss. Which of the following patient characteristics is a contraindication to its use?
A)      Congenital color vision deficiency
B)      History of heavy menstrual bleeding
C)       Mild elevation of serum aminotransferase concentrations
D)      Type 1 von Willebrand disease
E)      Allergy to milk protein
The correct response is Option A.
Tranexamic acid is a lysine analog that competitively blocks the lysine-binding sites of plasminogen, preventing its conversion to plasmin and thereby inhibiting fibrinolysis. In plastic surgery it has been shown to reduce blood loss in craniosynostosis and orthognathic surgery, reduction mammaplasty, face lift, rhinoplasty, and liposuction, and it may be given intravenously, topically, or mixed with local infiltration.
Transient disturbances of color vision have been reported with prolonged administration, and monitoring for this effect is part of standard toxicity surveillance. Because a patient with congenital color blindness cannot be monitored for a change, the manufacturer lists color blindness as a contraindication — a detail that is a favorite examination point precisely because it is counterintuitive.
Other genuine contraindications are known hypersensitivity to the drug, active intravascular clotting, subarachnoid hemorrhage, and a history of arterial or venous thromboembolism; renal impairment requires dose adjustment.
The remaining options are distractors of specific types. Heavy menstrual bleeding (Option B) is the drug’s FDA-approved indication, not a contraindication. Von Willebrand disease (Option D) is a condition in which tranexamic acid is therapeutically useful. Mildly elevated transaminases (Option C) and milk allergy (Option E) — the latter borrowed from aprotinin’s bovine origin — are not contraindications.
Question 6
A 58-year-old man in the intensive care unit 3 days after debridement of a necrotizing soft-tissue infection has a temperature of 39.1°C, a heart rate of 124 beats/min, and a blood pressure of 78/42 mmHg that does not improve after 30 mL/kg of balanced crystalloid. Serum lactate concentration is 4.6 mmol/L. Which of the following vasoactive agents is the most appropriate first-line therapy?
A)      Dopamine
B)      Norepinephrine
C)       Phenylephrine
D)      Vasopressin
E)      Dobutamine
The correct response is Option B.
Norepinephrine is the recommended first-line vasopressor in septic shock. Its predominantly alpha-1 agonist activity with modest beta-1 effect restores mean arterial pressure through vasoconstriction while largely preserving cardiac output and heart rate. Compared with dopamine (Option A), it produces significantly fewer arrhythmic events and, in the septic shock subgroup, is associated with lower mortality — dopamine is now reserved for highly selected patients with bradycardia and low arrhythmia risk. Vasopressin (Option D) is added as a second agent to reduce norepinephrine requirements, not started first. Phenylephrine (Option C) is a pure alpha agonist that reflexively reduces cardiac output and is a niche agent. Dobutamine (Option E) is an inotrope, appropriate for persistent hypoperfusion with evidence of myocardial dysfunction after volume and vasopressor optimization, not for initial blood pressure support.
The rest of the bundle matters as much as the vasopressor choice: obtain blood cultures and lactate, administer broad-spectrum antibiotics within the first hour, give 30 mL/kg of crystalloid for hypotension or lactate above 4 mmol/L, target a mean arterial pressure of 65 mmHg, remeasure lactate to gauge response, and — decisively in this patient — return to the operating room for source control if the necrotizing infection has not been fully debrided. Antibiotics and pressors cannot substitute for source control.
Question 7
A 44-year-old man with 55% total body surface area burns and inhalation injury develops bilateral pulmonary infiltrates, a PaO2/FiO2 ratio of 140, and no evidence of cardiogenic pulmonary edema on hospital day 4. Which of the following ventilator strategies is most appropriate?
A)      Tidal volume of 12 mL/kg predicted body weight with a plateau pressure limit of 40 cm H2O
B)      Tidal volume of 6 mL/kg predicted body weight with a plateau pressure goal below 30 cm H2O
C)       Pressure support ventilation with no set tidal volume
D)      High-frequency oscillatory ventilation as first-line therapy
E)      Tidal volume titrated to normalize PaCO2 regardless of plateau pressure
The correct response is Option B.
This patient meets the Berlin criteria for moderate acute respiratory distress syndrome: acute onset within a week of a known insult, bilateral opacities not fully explained by effusion or collapse, respiratory failure not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload, and a PaO2/FiO2 ratio between 100 and 200 with at least 5 cm H2O of positive end-expiratory pressure.
Lung-protective ventilation is the intervention with the clearest mortality benefit: tidal volumes of 6 mL/kg predictedbody weight (calculated from height and sex, not actual weight) with plateau pressures kept below 30 cm H2O. The mechanism of benefit is reduction of ventilator-induced lung injury from overdistention and cyclic recruitment. Permissive hypercapnia is accepted as a consequence, which is why titrating tidal volume to normalize PaCO2 (Option E) and using large tidal volumes (Option A) are both harmful.
Adjuncts with supporting evidence include a conservative fluid strategy after resuscitation is complete, prone positioning for severe disease (PaO2/FiO2 below 150), and neuromuscular blockade in selected severe cases. High-frequency oscillatory ventilation (Option D) did not improve and may worsen outcomes in trials and is not first-line. Pressure support (Option C) is a weaning mode, inappropriate in the acute phase. In burn patients specifically, inhalation injury and the enormous resuscitation volumes both contribute, and judicious fluid management during resuscitation reduces the pulmonary consequences.
Question 8
A 30-year-old man with 65% total body surface area burns has received 22 L of crystalloid over 14 hours. He becomes progressively difficult to ventilate with rising peak airway pressures, his abdomen is tense and distended, urine output has fallen to 8 mL/hr, and his blood pressure is declining. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?
A)      Increase the crystalloid infusion rate to improve urine output
B)      Measure bladder pressure to assess intra-abdominal pressure
C)       Administer intravenous furosemide
D)      Obtain a CT of the abdomen and pelvis
E)      Begin continuous renal replacement therapy
The correct response is Option B.
The constellation of a tense abdomen, rising airway pressures, oliguria, and hemodynamic deterioration after massive crystalloid resuscitation is abdominal compartment syndrome — a well-recognized complication of “fluid creep” during burn resuscitation. The diagnosis is made by measuring intra-abdominal pressure, most practically through a bladder catheter with a small instilled volume, with the patient supine and the transducer zeroed at the midaxillary line. Intra-abdominal hypertension is a sustained pressure above 12 mmHg; abdominal compartment syndrome is a sustained pressure above 20 mmHg with new organ dysfunction, which this patient clearly has.
Management escalates from medical measures — sedation and analgesia, neuromuscular blockade, nasogastric and rectal decompression, avoidance of further volume loading, and diuresis or ultrafiltration if volume overloaded — to decompressive laparotomy for refractory cases. Escharotomy of a circumferential truncal burn should be performed if it is contributing to restriction.
Increasing crystalloid (Option A) is the intuitive but disastrous response, since the oliguria is caused by the compartment syndrome, not hypovolemia. Furosemide (Option C) and renal replacement (Option E) may have adjunctive roles but do not address the immediate mechanical problem or establish the diagnosis. CT (Option D) delays a diagnosis that is made at the bedside. Prevention is best: use urine output rather than formula to titrate, consider colloid rescue, and monitor the resuscitation volume against the predicted total.

A)      Packed red blood cells alone, with plasma given only for an international normalized ratio above 2.0
B)      Packed red blood cells, plasma, and platelets in an approximately 1:1:1 ratio
C)       Packed red blood cells with crystalloid in a 1:3 ratio
D)      Packed red blood cells with albumin
E)      Packed red blood cells with cryoprecipitate alone
The correct response is Option B.
Balanced resuscitation with red blood cells, plasma, and platelets in approximately equal ratios is the standard for massive hemorrhage, based on trial data showing improved hemostasis and reduced death from exsanguination compared with more red-cell-predominant strategies. The physiologic rationale is the trauma-induced coagulopathy that accompanies major hemorrhage — a combination of consumption, dilution, hypothermia, acidosis, and fibrinolysis — which is worsened by transfusing red cells and crystalloid alone (Options A and C).
Adjuncts and monitoring complete the picture: tranexamic acid given within 3 hours of injury reduces mortality in bleeding trauma patients; calcium must be repleted, since citrate in stored blood chelates ionized calcium and causes hypocalcemia that impairs both coagulation and myocardial contractility; hypothermia must be prevented with warmed fluids and active warming; and viscoelastic testing (thromboelastography or rotational thromboelastometry) can guide targeted component therapy where available. Fibrinogen replacement with cryoprecipitate or concentrate is added when fibrinogen falls below roughly 150 to 200 mg/dL, but as an adjunct rather than a substitute for balanced components (Option E).
Permissive hypotension until surgical control, minimization of crystalloid, and rapid definitive hemostasis remain the framework within which transfusion occurs. Albumin (Option D) has no role in acute hemorrhagic resuscitation.
Question 10
A 24-year-old man is admitted after prolonged extrication from a collapsed structure with crush injury to both lower extremities. Urine is dark brown. Serum creatine kinase concentration is 48,000 U/L, and urine dipstick is strongly positive for blood with no red blood cells on microscopy. Which of the following is the most important initial therapy?
A)      Aggressive intravenous crystalloid to maintain a high urine output
B)      Intravenous furosemide to force diuresis
C)       Immediate hemodialysis
D)      Intravenous mannitol as monotherapy
E)      Restriction of intravenous fluids to prevent volume overload
The correct response is Option A.
Rhabdomyolysis after crush injury releases myoglobin, which precipitates in renal tubules — particularly in acidic urine — and causes direct tubular toxicity, vasoconstriction, and obstruction. A dipstick positive for blood without red cells on microscopy is the classic clue that the pigment is myoglobin rather than hemoglobin.
The single most important intervention is early, aggressive volume expansion with isotonic crystalloid, begun as soon as possible — ideally during extrication — and titrated to a brisk urine output, commonly targeted at 200 to 300 mL/hr in adults. Volume restriction (Option E) is exactly wrong; these patients sequester enormous volumes in injured muscle and are profoundly hypovolemic. Urinary alkalinization with bicarbonate and mannitol are used variably and remain of uncertain benefit; neither substitutes for volume (Option D), and mannitol should be avoided in established oliguria. Loop diuretics (Option B) do not prevent injury and acidify the urine. Dialysis (Option C) is reserved for established renal failure with refractory hyperkalemia, acidosis, or volume overload, not for prophylaxis.
Two associated issues must be managed simultaneously: hyperkalemia, which is released from necrotic muscle and can be immediately life-threatening, along with hyperphosphatemia and hypocalcemia; and compartment syndrome, which requires serial examination and compartment pressure measurement, with fasciotomy for a delta pressure below 30 mmHg.
Trauma
Question 1
A 176-lb (80-kg) man is brought to the emergency department 1 hour after sustaining deep partial- and full-thickness burns to 40% of his total body surface area. Using the Parkland formula, which of the following is the appropriate volume of lactated Ringer solution to be delivered in the first 8 hours after injury?
A)      3200 mL
B)      4800 mL
C)       6400 mL
D)      9600 mL
E)      12,800 mL
The correct response is Option C.
The Parkland formula estimates 4 mL × body weight in kilograms × percentage of total body surface area burned as the crystalloid requirement for the first 24 hours, with half of that volume given in the first 8 hours from the time of injury and the remaining half over the subsequent 16 hours.
For this patient: 4 × 80 × 40 = 12,800 mL over 24 hours, of which half — 6400 mL — is given in the first 8 hours, at approximately 800 mL/hr, and the remaining 6400 mL over the next 16 hours at approximately 400 mL/hr.
Two points reliably generate errors. First, the clock starts at the time of injury, not the time of arrival, so any delay compresses the first-half volume into the remaining hours; if this patient arrived 1 hour after the burn, the first 6400 mL must be delivered over 7 hours, not 8. Second, fluid already given in the field is subtracted from the calculated requirement.
Only second- and third-degree burns are counted in the total body surface area estimate; superficial (first-degree) burns are excluded. Most importantly, the formula is a starting point only: the infusion is titrated hourly to a urine output of 0.5 mL/kg/hr in adults and 1 mL/kg/hr in children. Over-resuscitation — “fluid creep” — causes abdominal and extremity compartment syndromes, pulmonary edema, and conversion of burn depth.
Question 2
A 33-year-old woman with 45% total body surface area burns is 6 hours into resuscitation. Her urine output over the last 2 hours has been 12 mL/hr and 9 mL/hr. Heart rate is 128 beats/min and blood pressure is 104/62 mmHg. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?
A)      Administer a dose of furosemide
B)      Increase the crystalloid infusion rate
C)       Start a norepinephrine infusion
D)      Transfuse 2 units of packed red blood cells
E)      Continue the current rate, since blood pressure is adequate
The correct response is Option B.
Urine output is the principal bedside endpoint of burn resuscitation, targeted at 0.5 mL/kg/hr in adults and 1 mL/kg/hr in children and in patients with high-voltage electrical injury or myoglobinuria. Oliguria in the first 24 hours after a major burn nearly always reflects inadequate volume, given the massive capillary leak and interstitial sequestration that characterize the early post-burn period, and the appropriate response is to increase the crystalloid rate — commonly by roughly a third — and reassess hourly.
Blood pressure is a poor early endpoint in burn resuscitation (Option E), because young patients maintain pressure through vasoconstriction and tachycardia well into significant hypovolemia; the tachycardia here supports under-resuscitation. Diuretics (Option A) treat the number rather than the physiology and worsen hypovolemia; the exception is that they may be considered late in resuscitation in a patient who is clearly volume overloaded and still oliguric. Vasopressors (Option C) increase blood pressure while worsening tissue and burn wound perfusion and should not substitute for volume in the initial phase. Transfusion (Option D) is not indicated without hemorrhage or significant anemia.
Persistent oliguria despite escalating volume should prompt reassessment: is the burn size underestimated, is there an unrecognized injury with ongoing hemorrhage, is there myoglobinuria requiring a higher urine output target, or is abdominal compartment syndrome developing?
Question 3
A 45-year-old man has circumferential full-thickness burns of the right arm and forearm sustained 8 hours ago. The hand is cool with sluggish capillary refill, passive extension of the fingers elicits pain, and Doppler signals in the radial artery have become progressively weaker. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A)      Elevation and observation for 4 more hours
B)      Immediate escharotomy through the eschar along the medial and lateral aspects of the limb
C)       Immediate four-compartment fasciotomy of the forearm as the initial procedure
D)      Amputation
E)      Application of a topical antimicrobial and delayed excision
The correct response is Option B.
Circumferential full-thickness burns produce an inelastic, unyielding eschar; as edema accumulates beneath it during resuscitation, tissue pressure rises and perfusion fails. Escharotomy is an incision through the burned eschar only, into the subcutaneous fat, which allows the tissue to expand. It is performed at the bedside, is generally painless in truly full-thickness burns, and is indicated for progressive loss of distal Doppler signals, increasing pain, paresthesias, decreased capillary refill, or firm compartments. On an extremity the incisions run along the medial and lateral midaxial lines, with care to avoid the ulnar nerve at the elbow and the peroneal nerve at the fibular head, and are extended across joints as needed. Circumferential chest or truncal burns causing restricted ventilation require escharotomy as well.
Delay (Options A and E) risks irreversible ischemia. Fasciotomy (Option C) is a distinct, deeper procedure indicated when a true compartment syndrome exists beneath the fascia — most commonly with high-voltage electrical injury, associated trauma or fracture, or very deep thermal injury — and is performed if escharotomy does not restore perfusion, but it is not the first maneuver for an isolated circumferential thermal burn. Amputation (Option D) is not indicated in a limb that has not been given the chance to reperfuse.
Question 4
A 29-year-old man is rescued from a house fire. He has singed nasal vibrissae, carbonaceous sputum, hoarseness, and facial burns. He is awake with an oxygen saturation of 98% on room air by pulse oximetry, and the carboxyhemoglobin level is 22%. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A)      Observation with humidified oxygen by face mask
B)      Administration of 100% oxygen by nonrebreather and early endotracheal intubation
C)       Administration of hyperbaric oxygen before any airway intervention
D)      Bronchoscopy first, with intubation only if mucosal injury is confirmed
E)      Administration of prophylactic corticosteroids and antibiotics
The correct response is Option B.
Two problems coexist here, and both are addressed by the same initial action.
First, the constellation of facial burns, singed vibrissae, carbonaceous sputum, and hoarseness in a closed-space fire predicts progressive upper airway edema over the ensuing hours. Once edema develops, intubation becomes difficult or impossible, and a surgical airway in a burned neck is hazardous. Early elective intubation is therefore correct; waiting for stridor or respiratory distress (Options A and D) is the classic error.
Second, an elevated carboxyhemoglobin level confirms carbon monoxide exposure. Carbon monoxide binds hemoglobin with roughly 200 to 250 times the affinity of oxygen, shifts the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve leftward, and impairs cellular respiration. Critically, pulse oximetry is falsely reassuring because it cannot distinguish carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin — a normal saturation, as here, does not exclude severe poisoning. Treatment is 100% oxygen, which reduces the half-life of carboxyhemoglobin from roughly 4 hours on room air to about 1 hour. Hyperbaric oxygen (Option C) shortens it further and is considered in severe poisoning, pregnancy, or neurologic impairment, but it must never delay airway control or resuscitation.
Cyanide toxicity should also be suspected with persistent lactic acidosis after combustion of synthetic materials. Steroids and prophylactic antibiotics (Option E) are not indicated in inhalation injury.
Question 5
A 3-year-old child sustains scald burns. Which of the following best describes how body surface area estimation in this child differs from that in an adult?
A)      The head represents a smaller percentage and the lower extremities a larger percentage than in an adult
B)      The head represents a larger percentage and the lower extremities a smaller percentage than in an adult
C)       The trunk represents a larger percentage than in an adult, with the head and limbs unchanged
D)      The rule of nines applies unchanged at all ages
E)      The palm of the hand represents approximately 5% of body surface area at this age
The correct response is Option B.
Body proportions change substantially with growth, and burn size estimation must account for it. In an infant, the head accounts for roughly 18 to 19% of body surface area and each lower extremity roughly 13 to 14%, compared with 9% and 18% respectively in an adult. Approximately 1% shifts from the head to the lower extremities for each year of life until adult proportions are reached around age 10. The Lund-Browder chart incorporates these age-specific values and is the preferred tool in children, since the standard rule of nines (Option D) will substantially underestimate head involvement and overestimate leg involvement in a young child — errors that translate directly into resuscitation volume errors.
The patient’s palmar surface including the fingers approximates 1% of body surface area at any age and is a useful bedside tool for patchy burns (Option E misstates this figure).
Pediatric burn resuscitation differs in other respects worth knowing: maintenance fluid containing dextrose is added to the resuscitation volume because children have limited glycogen stores, the urine output target is 1 mL/kg/hr, hypothermia develops rapidly because of the high surface-area-to-mass ratio, and any scald pattern that is not consistent with the history — sharply demarcated immersion lines, sparing of flexion creases, stocking-glove distribution — requires evaluation for non-accidental injury.
Question 6
A 37-year-old electrician contacts a 12,000-volt line with his right hand and is thrown to the ground. Entry and exit wounds are small. He has intact distal pulses, but the forearm is tense, passive finger extension elicits severe pain, and urine is dark red-brown. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A)      Observation with serial examination and elevation
B)      Fasciotomy of the involved compartments with debridement of nonviable muscle, aggressive fluid resuscitation, and cardiac monitoring
C)       Escharotomy of the cutaneous wounds only
D)      Immediate amputation of the extremity
E)      Fluid restriction to avoid worsening the edema
The correct response is Option B.
High-voltage electrical injury is fundamentally a deep tissue injury with a deceptively small cutaneous footprint. Current travels preferentially through the paths of least resistance — nerves, blood vessels, and muscle — generating heat in proportion to tissue resistance and cross-sectional area, so the greatest damage occurs in the deep muscle adjacent to bone and in the narrowest portions of the limb. The visible entry and exit wounds bear no relationship to the extent of injury.
Compartment syndrome is common and, as here, is diagnosed clinically: tense compartments and pain with passive stretch, with pulses characteristically preserved until very late. Fasciotomy with debridement of necrotic muscle is required, along with planned reexploration, since muscle necrosis is often progressive.
The dark urine indicates myoglobinuria, requiring aggressive crystalloid — not restriction (Option E) — with a urine output target of 1 to 2 mL/kg/hr, which is higher than the standard thermal burn target. Cardiac monitoring is indicated for arrhythmias, and hyperkalemia from muscle necrosis must be anticipated.
Observation (Option A) and cutaneous escharotomy alone (Option C) fail to decompress the fascial compartments. Amputation (Option D) is sometimes ultimately necessary but is not the initial management before an attempt at decompression and serial debridement. Associated injuries — spine and long bone fractures from tetanic contraction or the fall, and cataracts and neurologic sequelae presenting later — should be sought.
Question 7
A 41-year-old laboratory technician spills concentrated hydrofluoric acid on his right hand. Two hours later he has severe, deep, throbbing pain out of proportion to the modest erythema visible on the skin. Which of the following is the most appropriate treatment after copious irrigation?
A)      Topical and intradermal calcium gluconate, with intra-arterial calcium gluconate if pain persists
B)      Neutralization with sodium bicarbonate solution
C)       Application of silver sulfadiazine and observation
D)      Topical magnesium sulfate paste
E)      Immediate tangential excision and skin grafting
The correct response is Option A.
Hydrofluoric acid causes injury by two mechanisms: the hydrogen ion produces a corrosive burn, but the far more dangerous effect is that the fluoride ion penetrates tissue and avidly chelates calcium and magnesium, causing liquefactive necrosis and cell death. The clinical signature is pain markedly out of proportion to the visible skin injury, often delayed by hours with dilute solutions.
Treatment is to bind free fluoride with calcium. After copious water irrigation, calcium gluconate gel is applied topically and massaged into the tissue; persistent pain is treated with local infiltration of 5 to 10% calcium gluconate (calcium chloride must not be injected because of tissue necrosis), and refractory cases — particularly involving the digits, where infiltration is limited by compartment constraints — are treated with intra-arterial calcium gluconate infusion into the radial or brachial artery, or with Bier block administration. Pain relief is the endpoint that guides therapy. Nail plate removal may be necessary for subungual involvement.
Systemic effects deserve equal attention: significant exposures produce hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, and hyperkalemia with fatal arrhythmias, so serum calcium and electrocardiographic monitoring are mandatory for large or concentrated exposures.
Neutralization (Option B) generates heat and is contraindicated for chemical burns generally. Options C, D, and E do not address the fluoride ion.
Question 8
A 22-year-old man sustains an open tibial fracture with a 12-cm soft-tissue defect exposing bone with periosteal stripping, following a motorcycle collision. The limb is well perfused with intact tibial nerve function. He has been taken for irrigation, debridement, and external fixation. Which of the following best describes the optimal timing of definitive soft-tissue coverage?
A)      Within 72 hours to 7 days of injury, after adequate debridement
B)      After 6 weeks of negative pressure wound therapy in all cases
C)       Only after the fracture has united
D)      At the time of the initial debridement in all cases, regardless of wound contamination
E)      Delayed indefinitely, with healing by secondary intention
The correct response is Option A.
This is a Gustilo-Anderson type IIIB open tibial fracture — extensive soft-tissue loss with periosteal stripping and bone exposure requiring flap coverage — distinguished from IIIA (adequate soft-tissue coverage despite extensive laceration) and IIIC (associated arterial injury requiring repair).
The classic teaching from Godina’s work is that early coverage, historically within 72 hours, yields lower rates of infection, flap failure, and nonunion than delayed coverage. Contemporary practice, aided by negative pressure wound therapy and staged debridement, supports a somewhat wider early window — generally within 7 days — with the essential caveat that coverage follows adequate debridement, not a calendar. The wound must be clean and all nonviable tissue removed before a flap is placed; covering contaminated or marginally viable tissue converts a soft-tissue problem into a deep infection under a flap (which is why Option D is wrong as an absolute).
Negative pressure therapy is a bridge, not a destination (Option B); prolonged reliance on it before coverage is associated with increasing colonization and worse outcomes. Waiting for union (Option C) is untenable with exposed bone. Secondary intention (Option E) will not close a 12-cm defect over denuded tibia.
The reconstructive plan is coordinated with orthopedics, and free tissue transfer is generally required for defects of the distal third.
Question 9
A 19-year-old man has increasing pain in the left leg 10 hours after a tibial shaft fracture treated with a splint. Examination shows a tense anterior compartment, pain with passive toe extension, and paresthesias in the first web space. Dorsalis pedis pulse is palpable. Compartment pressure in the anterior compartment measures 48 mmHg; his diastolic blood pressure is 72 mmHg. Which of the following is the most appropriate management?
A)      Split the splint, elevate the leg, and remeasure in 2 hours
B)      Emergent four-compartment fasciotomy of the leg
C)       Arteriography to exclude vascular injury
D)      Administration of mannitol and observation
E)      Elevation of the limb above the level of the heart with serial examinations
The correct response is Option B.
The diagnosis of acute compartment syndrome is primarily clinical, and this patient has the two most useful findings — pain out of proportion and pain with passive stretch of the muscles within the compartment — together with paresthesias in the deep peroneal nerve distribution. Pulselessness is a late and unreliable sign, since compartment pressure rarely exceeds systolic arterial pressure; a palpable pulse never excludes the diagnosis.
Pressure measurement supports the diagnosis when the examination is unreliable — the obtunded, intoxicated, or regionally anesthetized patient. The most useful threshold is the delta pressure: diastolic blood pressure minus compartment pressure, with a value below 30 mmHg indicating fasciotomy. Here the delta pressure is 72 − 48 = 24 mmHg.
Treatment is emergent fasciotomy releasing all four compartments of the leg, usually through a two-incision technique with a lateral incision for the anterior and lateral compartments and a medial incision for the superficial and deep posterior compartments. Muscle necrosis begins within hours, and delay produces contracture, neurologic deficit, and rhabdomyolysis with renal failure.
Splitting the cast and elevating to the level of the heart are appropriate temporizing measures while preparing for the operating room, but they are not definitive (Option A), and elevating above the heart (Option E) reduces arterial inflow and perfusion pressure and is counterproductive. Arteriography (Option C) and mannitol (Option D) delay definitive treatment.
Question 10
A 28-year-old man is brought in after being lost overnight in subfreezing conditions. His fingers are hard, white, insensate, and frozen. He is hemodynamically stable and his core temperature is 35.6°C. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial management of the digits?
A)      Rewarming in circulating water at 37 to 39°C until thawing is complete, with analgesia
B)      Rubbing the digits with snow and gradual passive rewarming at ambient temperature
C)       Rewarming with dry radiant heat from a heat lamp
D)      Immediate debridement of all insensate tissue
E)      Delayed rewarming until the patient can be transferred to a burn center 12 hours away
The correct response is Option A.
Rapid rewarming in gently circulating water at 37 to 39°C, continued until the tissue is pliable and erythematous — typically 20 to 40 minutes — is the standard initial treatment of frostbite and requires substantial parenteral analgesia, since thawing is intensely painful. Two principles govern the decision: rewarming should be rapid, not gradual, and it should not be initiated if there is any risk of refreezing, because freeze-thaw-refreeze cycles cause far greater tissue destruction than remaining frozen during transport. Dry heat (Option C) causes burns in insensate tissue, and friction or rubbing with snow (Option B) mechanically destroys ice-crystal-laden tissue; both are historical practices that cause harm.
After rewarming, care includes elevation, splinting, avoidance of pressure and further trauma, tetanus prophylaxis, ibuprofen for thromboxane inhibition, topical aloe vera, and selective drainage or debridement of clear blisters while leaving hemorrhagic blisters intact. Angiography or technetium scanning within the first 24 hours can identify perfusion deficits, and thrombolytic therapy with tPA, given within roughly 24 hours of thawing in patients without contraindications, has been shown to reduce digital amputation rates substantially; iloprost is used similarly where available.
Surgical management follows the maxim “frostbite in January, amputate in July” — definitive debridement and amputation are deliberately delayed for weeks to months until demarcation is complete (Option D), except when infection or wet gangrene forces earlier intervention.
Transplantation
Question 1
A 34-year-old man undergoes bilateral hand transplantation. Immediately after release of the vascular clamps, the allografts become mottled and cyanotic, and no arterial inflow can be reestablished despite technically satisfactory anastomoses. Which of the following best describes the mechanism of this event?
A)      T-cell-mediated response to donor major histocompatibility antigens
B)      Preformed recipient antibodies against donor antigens with complement activation and microvascular thrombosis
C)       Chronic antibody- and cell-mediated intimal hyperplasia
D)      Donor immune cells attacking recipient tissues
E)      Ischemia-reperfusion injury without an immunologic component
The correct response is Option B.
Hyperacute rejection is a humoral event mediated by antibodies already present in the recipient at the time of transplantation — typically from prior transplantation, transfusion, or pregnancy — directed against donor ABO or human leukocyte antigens. On reperfusion, these antibodies bind donor endothelium, activate complement, and produce diffuse microvascular thrombosis and immediate ischemic graft failure within minutes to hours. It is untreatable once it occurs, so prevention through ABO matching, crossmatching, and panel-reactive antibody screening is the entire strategy.
The remaining categories are worth holding as a set. Accelerated rejection occurs on days 2 to 5, is mediated by memory cells in a presensitized recipient, and is essentially a delayed variant of the same humoral process. Acute rejection(Option A) is T-cell mediated, occurs any time after roughly the fifth postoperative day and most commonly within the first 4 to 6 months, and generally responds to augmented immunosuppression. Chronic rejection (Option C) is a combined antibody- and cell-mediated process producing progressive arterial intimal hyperplasia and fibrosis over months to years. Graft-versus-host disease (Option D) is the reverse direction — donor immune cells attacking recipient tissue — and is characteristic of bone marrow and stem cell transplantation. Ischemia-reperfusion injury (Option E) is real but would not cause failure to reperfuse technically patent anastomoses.
Question 2
A 41-year-old woman is 5 months post hand transplantation. She reports a mildly pruritic, erythematous maculopapular eruption over the dorsum of the transplanted hand and forearm. Motor function and sensation are unchanged, and she is afebrile. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?
A)      Reassurance and observation
B)      Skin biopsy of the graft for histologic grading
C)       Empiric treatment with oral acyclovir
D)      Immediate reduction of immunosuppression
E)      Amputation of the allograft
The correct response is Option B.
Skin is the most antigenic component of a vascularized composite allograft and is the first tissue to manifest rejection, which is why the cutaneous surface serves as a uniquely accessible “sentinel” not available in solid organ transplantation. The typical presentation of acute rejection is exactly as described: an erythematous, sometimes maculopapular or blotchy eruption over the graft, often preceding any functional change. Nearly all hand transplant recipients experience at least one episode of acute rejection in the first year.
Because the differential includes infection, drug eruption, and contact dermatitis, and because treatment intensity is graded by severity, the appropriate step is a punch biopsy of graft skin with histologic grading using the Banff classification for skin-containing composite tissue allografts, which grades from 0 (no rejection) through IV (necrotizing acute rejection) on the basis of the perivascular infiltrate, epidermal involvement, and dyskeratosis.
Observation (Option A) permits progression. Antivirals (Option C) treat a different diagnosis. Reducing immunosuppression (Option D) is precisely backward. Graft amputation (Option E) is a last resort, reserved for uncontrolled rejection, graft loss, or life-threatening complications of immunosuppression. Most acute episodes are treated with pulse corticosteroids plus topical tacrolimus and clobetasol applied to the graft skin, with augmentation of maintenance therapy for refractory cases.
Question 3
A patient is scheduled to undergo face transplantation. Which of the following best describes the standard immunosuppressive strategy for vascularized composite allotransplantation?
A)      Induction with a lymphocyte-depleting or interleukin-2 receptor antagonist agent, followed by maintenance with tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and corticosteroids
B)      Corticosteroid monotherapy indefinitely
C)       Cyclosporine monotherapy with no induction
D)      Induction only, with no maintenance therapy required after the first year
E)      Sirolimus monotherapy begun on the day of surgery
The correct response is Option A.
Vascularized composite allotransplantation has adopted the immunosuppressive framework of solid organ transplantation: induction followed by lifelong triple maintenance therapy. Induction uses either a lymphocyte-depleting agent — antithymocyte globulin or alemtuzumab — or an interleukin-2 receptor antagonist such as basiliximab, given at the time of transplantation to blunt the intense early alloresponse. Maintenance is typically a calcineurin inhibitor (tacrolimus), an antiproliferative agent (mycophenolate mofetil), and low-dose corticosteroids, with topical agents added to graft skin during cutaneous rejection episodes.
The requirement for lifelong multidrug immunosuppression is the central ethical tension of these procedures. Unlike a kidney or heart transplant, a hand or face transplant is life-enhancing rather than life-saving, and its benefits must be weighed against the cumulative toxicity of immunosuppression — opportunistic infection, nephrotoxicity, diabetes, hypertension, and elevated long-term risk of malignancy including post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder and cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma. This calculus is central to candidate selection and consent, and it drives research into tolerance induction protocols such as donor bone marrow infusion.
Monotherapy regimens (Options B, C, and E) are inadequate for the highly antigenic skin component, immunosuppression cannot be discontinued (Option D), and sirolimus in particular impairs wound healing and is avoided perioperatively.
Question 4
A 29-year-old man is 3 weeks post face transplantation. He develops white, curd-like plaques over the buccal mucosa and tongue of the allograft, with mild discomfort. He is afebrile, the graft is well perfused, and there is no cutaneous erythema. Which of the following is the most appropriate initial management?
A)      Increase the tacrolimus dose
B)      Topical nystatin or clotrimazole
C)       Intravenous amphotericin B
D)      Oral amoxicillin
E)      Punch biopsy of the graft skin with pulse corticosteroids
The correct response is Option B.
Facial allotransplantation is distinctive among vascularized composite allografts in that the graft includes oral and sinonasal mucosa colonized with native flora, and Candida species are the most common organisms encountered. Superficial oropharyngeal candidiasis in an immunosuppressed recipient is treated with topical antifungal therapy — nystatin suspension or clotrimazole troches — which is effective, avoids systemic toxicity, and avoids the interaction between azoles and calcineurin inhibitors that can sharply raise tacrolimus levels.
Systemic amphotericin (Option C) is reserved for invasive or refractory disease. Antibacterial therapy (Option D) does not treat a fungal infection. Increasing tacrolimus (Option A) treats rejection, which this presentation does not describe: the graft is well perfused without cutaneous erythema, and mucosal plaques are not a rejection pattern. Biopsy with pulse steroids (Option E) would be appropriate if there were signs of cutaneous rejection, and biopsy is reasonable if the lesions fail to respond to topical therapy — but escalating immunosuppression in the face of an untreated infection is the error the question is testing.
Infection prophylaxis in these recipients is comprehensive, including cytomegalovirus prophylaxis with valganciclovir, Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and antifungal prophylaxis, with the highest cytomegalovirus risk in a seronegative recipient of a seropositive donor graft.
Question 5
A 46-year-old hand transplant recipient maintained on tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and prednisone is found to have a serum creatinine concentration that has risen from 0.9 to 1.8 mg/dL over 8 months. He also reports a fine hand tremor and has a new diagnosis of diabetes mellitus. Which of the following is the most likely explanation?
A)      Chronic rejection of the allograft
B)      Calcineurin inhibitor toxicity
C)       Mycophenolate mofetil toxicity
D)      Corticosteroid-induced myopathy
E)      Post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder
The correct response is Option B.
Nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity, and new-onset diabetes after transplantation form the characteristic toxicity triad of calcineurin inhibitors, of which tacrolimus is the most widely used. Renal injury is dose- and duration-dependent, arising from afferent arteriolar vasoconstriction acutely and chronic interstitial fibrosis over time; progressive renal dysfunction is one of the most common long-term complications in vascularized composite allotransplantation — a serious consideration given that these are non-life-saving procedures in otherwise healthy recipients. Neurotoxicity ranges from tremor and headache to seizures and posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome. Tacrolimus is more diabetogenic than cyclosporine, whereas cyclosporine causes more hirsutism and gingival hyperplasia.
Management involves trough level monitoring, dose minimization, and sometimes conversion to a mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitor, tempered by that class’s impairment of wound healing. Drug interactions matter clinically: azole antifungals, macrolides, and calcium channel blockers raise levels, while rifampin and several anticonvulsants lower them.
Mycophenolate toxicity (Option C) is principally gastrointestinal and hematologic. Chronic rejection (Option A) manifests in the graft — intimal hyperplasia, skin and adnexal atrophy, fibrosis, loss of function — not as systemic renal failure. Post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (Option E) presents with lymphadenopathy or mass lesions and is Epstein-Barr virus driven.
Question 6
A 52-year-old kidney transplant recipient maintained on sirolimus, tacrolimus, and prednisone is scheduled to undergo excision of a lower extremity soft-tissue mass with local flap closure. Which of the following perioperative medication adjustments should be discussed with the transplant team?
A)      Conversion from sirolimus to an alternative agent because of impaired wound healing
B)      Discontinuation of all immunosuppression for 2 weeks perioperatively
C)       Doubling the prednisone dose for 30 days postoperatively
D)      Discontinuation of tacrolimus because of its antiproliferative effect on fibroblasts
E)      No adjustment is warranted, as immunosuppressants do not affect wound healing
The correct response is Option A.
Mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitors — sirolimus and everolimus — have a well-documented adverse effect on wound healing. By inhibiting the mTOR pathway they suppress fibroblast proliferation, collagen deposition, and vascular endothelial growth factor-mediated angiogenesis, producing higher rates of wound dehiscence, lymphocele, incisional hernia, and delayed healing. The standard recommendation is to avoid these agents in the immediate perioperative period, either by delaying their introduction after transplantation or by temporarily converting to a calcineurin inhibitor-based regimen around elective surgery, coordinated with the transplant team.
Corticosteroids also impair healing, and perioperative vitamin A supplementation can partially counteract that effect; doses should not be increased beyond what is required (Option C), though stress-dose coverage is considered for adrenal suppression. Tacrolimus and mycophenolate have comparatively less impact on healing (Option D). Withdrawing immunosuppression entirely (Option B) risks acute rejection of the kidney and is never appropriate.
Two other considerations belong in the plan for any transplant recipient undergoing elective plastic surgery: heightened vigilance for infection with atypical organisms, and screening for cutaneous malignancy, since chronically immunosuppressed patients develop squamous cell carcinoma at many times the rate of the general population.
Question 7
A 58-year-old renal transplant recipient of 11 years is referred for evaluation of multiple scaly, keratotic lesions of the scalp, ears, and dorsal hands. Biopsy of two lesions shows well-differentiated squamous cell carcinoma. Which of the following statements about cutaneous malignancy in this population is most accurate?
A)      The incidence is comparable to that of the general population
B)      Basal cell carcinoma outnumbers squamous cell carcinoma, as in the general population
C)       Squamous cell carcinomas are more numerous, more aggressive, and more likely to metastasize than in immunocompetent patients
D)      Melanoma is the predominant skin cancer in this population
E)      These lesions require no different management than in an immunocompetent patient
The correct response is Option C.
Chronic immunosuppression profoundly increases the risk of nonmelanoma skin cancer, with reported increases on the order of 65- to 100-fold for squamous cell carcinoma. Two features distinguish this population. First, the usual ratio is inverted: squamous cell carcinoma outnumbers basal cell carcinoma by roughly 4:1, the reverse of the general population (Option B). Second, the tumors behave more aggressively — arising in greater numbers, recurring locally more often, and metastasizing at substantially higher rates, particularly on the head and neck and with longer duration of immunosuppression. Human papillomavirus co-infection and the direct carcinogenic effects of certain agents, notably azathioprine and calcineurin inhibitors, contribute.
Management reflects this: treatment with clear margins and a low threshold for Mohs surgery, rigorous surveillance with total-body skin examinations at least annually and often every 3 to 6 months in patients with prior cancers, strict photoprotection counseling, field treatment of actinic damage, consideration of oral retinoid or nicotinamide chemoprophylaxis, and — importantly — discussion with the transplant team about reducing immunosuppression or converting to a mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitor, which has an antineoplastic effect and is associated with fewer subsequent skin cancers. Option E therefore understates the required intensity, and Options A and D are factually incorrect.
Question 8
Two years after hand transplantation, a recipient has gradual loss of intrinsic function, thinning and atrophy of the graft skin, loss of hair and sweating within the allograft, and progressive stiffness. Angiography shows diffuse narrowing and intimal thickening of the graft vessels. Which of the following best describes this process?
A)      Hyperacute rejection
B)      Acute cellular rejection
C)       Chronic rejection with graft vasculopathy
D)      Graft-versus-host disease
E)      Reperfusion injury
The correct response is Option C.
Chronic rejection in vascularized composite allotransplantation manifests as a graft vasculopathy analogous to cardiac allograft vasculopathy or bronchiolitis obliterans in lung transplantation: an indolent, combined antibody- and cell-mediated process producing concentric intimal hyperplasia and luminal narrowing of graft vessels, with downstream fibrosis and atrophy. The clinical picture is exactly as described — skin and adnexal atrophy with loss of hair and sweating, myointimal proliferation on imaging, muscle atrophy, joint stiffness, and progressive functional decline over months to years. Repeated episodes of acute rejection are the strongest predictor of its development, which is the principal argument for aggressively treating every acute episode.
There is no reliably effective treatment once it is established; management focuses on optimizing immunosuppression and ultimately on graft removal when function is lost.
Hyperacute rejection (Option A) occurs within minutes to hours of reperfusion. Acute cellular rejection (Option B) presents with an erythematous cutaneous eruption and a perivascular lymphocytic infiltrate on biopsy, and typically responds to augmented immunosuppression. Graft-versus-host disease (Option D) is donor immune cells attacking recipient tissue, uncommon in vascularized composite allotransplantation. Reperfusion injury (Option E) is an early, nonimmunologic event.
Question 9
A 36-year-old face transplant recipient develops fever, cervical lymphadenopathy, and a mediastinal mass 14 months after transplantation. He was Epstein-Barr virus seronegative before transplantation and received a graft from a seropositive donor. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?
A)      Acute rejection
B)      Post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder
C)       Cytomegalovirus syndrome
D)      Disseminated tuberculosis
E)      Chronic rejection
The correct response is Option B.
Post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder is a spectrum of Epstein-Barr virus-driven B-cell proliferations ranging from polyclonal hyperplasia to monomorphic lymphoma, arising when immunosuppression impairs the T-cell surveillance that normally restrains virus-infected B cells. The highest-risk group is precisely this one: an Epstein-Barr virus-seronegative recipient of a seropositive donor graft, in whom primary infection occurs under full immunosuppression. Risk is also increased by more intense immunosuppression, particularly lymphocyte-depleting induction agents, and most cases present within the first 1 to 2 years.
Presentation is protean — fever, lymphadenopathy, a mass lesion, gastrointestinal symptoms, or graft dysfunction — and diagnosis requires tissue biopsy with immunophenotyping and Epstein-Barr virus studies, supported by serial viral load monitoring. The cornerstone of treatment is reduction of immunosuppression, with rituximab for CD20-positive disease and chemotherapy for monomorphic or refractory disease.
Cytomegalovirus syndrome (Option C) produces fever, leukopenia, and end-organ disease and is typically prevented by valganciclovir prophylaxis; the seronegative recipient of a seropositive donor is likewise the highest-risk group for cytomegalovirus, a useful parallel to remember. Rejection (Options A and E) manifests in the graft, not as systemic lymphadenopathy with a mediastinal mass. Tuberculosis (Option D) is possible in an immunosuppressed patient but far less likely given the specific serologic mismatch described.
Question 10
Which of the following best describes an appropriate candidate for face transplantation?
A)      A patient with a defect readily reconstructed with conventional autologous techniques who prefers a single operation
B)      A patient with an extensive central facial defect involving multiple aesthetic and functional subunits that cannot be reconstructed conventionally, who is psychosocially stable, adherent, and fully informed of the risks of lifelong immunosuppression
C)       A patient with active substance use disorder and untreated major depression, whose defect is severe
D)      A patient with a recently treated metastatic malignancy
E)      A patient with an isolated nasal defect
The correct response is Option B.
Candidate selection is the defining issue in facial allotransplantation, because the procedure is life-enhancing rather than life-saving and commits an otherwise healthy patient to lifelong immunosuppression with its attendant risks of infection, nephrotoxicity, metabolic disease, and malignancy. The appropriate candidate has a defect of such magnitude — typically central facial, with loss of multiple functional and aesthetic subunits such as the nose, lips, and oral sphincter — that conventional autologous reconstruction cannot restore acceptable form or function. A patient whose defect can be addressed with established techniques (Options A and E) should be reconstructed conventionally, since the risk-benefit calculus does not favor transplantation.
Beyond the defect itself, selection requires rigorous multidisciplinary evaluation: psychiatric and psychological assessment for stability, realistic expectations, and the capacity to cope with an altered identity and with public attention; demonstrated adherence, since nonadherence with immunosuppression leads to graft loss; adequate social support; absence of active substance use disorder or untreated psychiatric illness (Option C); and freedom from active malignancy and uncontrolled infection (Option D). Immunologic screening, imaging of the recipient vasculature, and detailed informed consent addressing graft loss and the possibility of returning to a worse baseline complete the workup.
